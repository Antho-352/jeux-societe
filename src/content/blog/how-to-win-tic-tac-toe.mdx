Most tic-tac-toe advice stops at "take the centre". That is not enough to stop a fork, and it is not how the first player actually wins. Below is the full priority list that never loses, the corner trap that punishes seven of the eight possible replies, the only safe replies when you go second, and six positions to test yourself. There is a computer to practise against at the end.

## Can you always win tic-tac-toe?

**No, and neither can anyone else.** Tic-tac-toe is a solved game: if both players make no mistakes, every game ends in a draw. Mathematicians call it a futile game for exactly that reason. Computers have played it perfectly since 1952, when Sandy Douglas wrote OXO for the EDSAC computer at Cambridge, one of the first known video games.

What you can do is **never lose, and win every game in which your opponent slips**. Against children and casual players, that is often enough to come out ahead.

## The priority list: what to play on every move

The surest way to play well is to **check the same eight rules every turn, in this order, and play the first one that applies**. Allen Newell and Herbert Simon used this list in their 1972 tic-tac-toe program, and following it gives a win or at least a draw.

1. **Win.** If you have two in a row with the third square empty, play there.
2. **Block.** If your opponent has two in a row, take the third square.
3. **Fork.** Make a move that creates two lines of two at once. Your opponent can block only one of them.
4. **Block their fork.** If your opponent can fork next turn, take that square, or force them to block you with a two in a row that does not hand them the fork.
5. **Centre.** Take the middle square.
6. **Opposite corner.** If your opponent is in a corner, take the corner diagonally opposite.
7. **Empty corner.** Take any corner.
8. **Empty edge.** Take a middle square on one of the sides.

Rules 1 and 2 are the ones everyone knows. **Rules 3 and 4 are where games are won and lost.** A fork is the only way to beat an opponent who always blocks, so learning to spot it, both for you and against you, is the whole game.

## How to win when you go first: the corner trap

**Open in a corner.** Against perfect play the centre and the corner both draw, but after a corner opening seven of the eight replies lose by force. Only the centre holds. Here is how X wins against each kind of reply.

### If O answers on an edge

Take the centre. O must block the diagonal, and you take a corner that makes a fork.

  boards={[

    { cells: 'XO..X...O', highlight: [5], caption: 'X takes the centre; O must block.' },

The same idea works when O plays the edge further away: take the centre, wait for the block, then take the corner that lines up with both of your Xs.

### If O answers in a corner

Take another corner so that O has to block between your two Xs, then take the last free corner.

    { cells: 'X.OO..X..', highlight: [7], caption: 'X takes the bottom-left corner; O must block in between.' },
    { cells: 'X.OO..X.X', highlight: [9], caption: 'X takes the last corner: bottom row and diagonal.' },
  ]}
  caption="If O takes the corner opposite yours instead, take one of the two remaining corners; O blocks between your Xs, and the last corner forks again."
/>

### If O answers in the centre

This is the correct reply, so you cannot force a win, but you can set one more trap. **Take the corner opposite your first X.** Now O must play an edge. If O takes a corner, you are forced to block, and the block itself makes a fork.

<Callout type="tip" title="Why the corner works">
A corner sits on three lines (a row, a column and a diagonal), an edge on only two. After a corner opening just one reply is safe, against four after a centre or edge opening, so the corner sets the most traps for an opponent who does not know the answer.
</Callout>

## How never to lose when you go second

As O you are playing for a draw, and you get it by **answering the first move correctly**. Any other answer lets a perfect X force a win.

Then watch for the two traps that catch second players after a good start:

- **X in two opposite corners, you in the centre:** play an edge, not a corner. An edge makes X block you, and X's forced block does not create a fork. A corner forces X into exactly the square that forks.
- **X in the centre and the corner opposite yours:** here it is the reverse. Take one of the two free corners. Any edge loses: X replies in a corner that threatens two lines at once.

After the opening, go back to the priority list. Win, block, stop forks; the draw follows.

## Tic-tac-toe tricks that beat beginners

These are the habits that win in real games, where nobody plays perfectly.

- **Always open in a corner as X.** Seven of the eight replies lose, so an opponent who does not know the answer is likely to pick a losing one.
- **If you open in the centre and they answer on an edge, take a corner next to their O.** They must block the diagonal, and your next corner forks. A centre opening is drawn against a corner reply, so this only works against an edge.
- **Count lines, not squares.** Before each move, run your eye over the eight lines (three rows, three columns, two diagonals) and look for any line with two of the same mark and a space.
- **Alternate who starts.** Our [tic-tac-toe rules](/rules/tic-tac-toe/) suggest swapping the first player after each game, because starting is an advantage.
- **Change the game when it goes stale.** In misère tic-tac-toe, the player who makes three in a row loses. It is still a draw with perfect play, but nobody's instincts work any more.

## Practise against the computer

The beginner computer only takes a win or blocks a line; otherwise it plays at random. **Beat it with a fork from a corner opening.** Then switch to the perfect computer: it searches every possible game, so the best you can get is a draw.

## When tic-tac-toe gets too easy: Sim, dots and boxes

Once both players block every fork, every game is a draw and it is time for a pencil game that is not solved at the table. Our [pen-and-paper rules](/rules/pen-and-paper/) have several; these are the best next steps.

**Sim** cannot end in a draw at all. Six dots, fifteen lines, two colours, and the first player to complete a triangle in their own colour loses. A computer search in 1974 showed that the second player can always win with perfect play, but few people know how, so at the table it is still a real fight.

<ItemCallout href="/rules/sim/" note="Six dots and two coloured pens. A two-minute game where somebody always loses." />

**Dots and boxes**, first published by the French mathematician Édouard Lucas in the 19th century, rewards the same habit as forks: thinking one move ahead about what you give away. Close a box and you play again, so the endgame turns on long chains of boxes.

<ItemCallout href="/rules/dots-and-boxes/" note="Rules, the chain and double-cross strategy, and a printable sheet of four 6×6 dot grids." />

If you like the three-in-a-row idea, [three men's morris](/board-games/printable-three-mens-morris/) adds movement: each player has only three pieces and slides them once all six are placed. [Connect Four](/rules/connect-four/) goes the other way: the first player can force a win, but the winning line is far too long to memorise. For a longer game on the same theme, try [nine men's morris](/rules/nine-mens-morris/) or the pencil game [Sprouts](/rules/sprouts/), played with dots and lines.

You cannot force a win at tic-tac-toe, but if you **win, block, fork and stop forks before anything else**, open in a corner as X and answer correctly as O, you will never lose.
</Callout>
