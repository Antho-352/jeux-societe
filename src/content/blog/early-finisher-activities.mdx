Every class has them: the students who finish ten minutes before everyone else and ask "what do I do now?". Early finisher activities answer that question before it is asked. The best ones are quiet, need no explanation, let students check their own work, and are short enough to finish. Below are 20 free, no-prep printables sorted by grade, from kindergarten to middle school, with a choice board and a plan for an early finisher station you can set up in an afternoon.

## What makes a good early finisher activity

**An early finisher task has to work without you.** You are still teaching the rest of the class, so it must pass four tests:

1. **Quiet.** One student, or two at most, and no talking across the room.
2. **No new instructions.** The student knows what to do from the page alone.
3. **Self-checking.** An answer key lets the student see whether they got it right without asking you.
4. **Short.** One page that can be finished in the time left, so nobody leaves a half-done task.

One-page puzzles pass all four. Every puzzle in this list is a free PDF from our site with the answer or the finished picture on the last page, in black and white on US Letter paper so it photocopies cleanly. There is no sign-up and nothing to buy.

## Early finisher activities for K–2

**Young children need pictures, short words and big spaces.** Pick tasks that need little or no reading and keep the pencil moving.

1. **[Kindergarten sight words word search](/puzzles/word-search/kindergarten-sight-words-word-search/).** Fifteen sight words (the, and, said, like, look) in a 10 × 10 grid, reading only across or down. Ages 4 to 6.
2. **[Color word search](/puzzles/word-search/color-word-search/).** Fifteen color names from red to violet, across or down only. Ages 4 to 7.
3. **[Star maze](/puzzles/mazes/star-maze/).** A five-pointed star with thick walls and a short path, solution on page 2. Ages 4 to 7.
4. **[Easy rocket maze](/puzzles/mazes/rocket-maze-easy/).** From the nose cone down to the launch pad. Ages 4 to 7.
5. **[Alphabet butterfly dot to dot](/puzzles/dot-to-dot/abc-butterfly-dot-to-dot/).** Join the letters in alphabetical order and a butterfly appears: letter practice that does not feel like it. Kindergarten and first grade.
6. **[4×4 sudoku for kids](/puzzles/sudoku/4x4-sudoku-for-kids/).** Sixty first sudoku grids with the numbers 1 to 4, six per page. Ages 6 to 8.
7. **[Weather word search](/puzzles/word-search/weather-word-search/).** Fifteen weather words, from drizzle to thunder, across or down. Ages 5 to 8.

## Early finisher activities for grades 3–5

**From about 8 to 11, students can handle vocabulary, logic and longer mazes.** Tie the word searches to the current unit when you can.

8. **[6×6 sudoku for kids](/puzzles/sudoku/6x6-sudoku-for-kids/).** Forty puzzles with 2 × 3 boxes and the numbers 1 to 6, solvable without guessing, four per page. The step between 4×4 and a full 9×9. Ages 8 to 12.
9. **[Math word search](/puzzles/word-search/math-word-search/).** Twenty math words, from addition to perimeter, in a 15 × 15 grid. A vocabulary review that fits the end of a math lesson. Ages 9 to 12.
10. **[Space maze](/puzzles/mazes/space-maze/).** A tall rocket-shaped maze with long corridors and deep dead ends. Ages 8 to 12.
11. **[Dragon maze](/puzzles/mazes/dragon-maze/).** A 20 × 20 square maze where the knight must reach the dragon. Ages 8 to 12.
12. **[Dinosaur word search](/puzzles/word-search/dinosaur-word-search/).** Eighteen dinosaur words, triceratops and stegosaurus included. Ages 7 to 10.
13. **[Human body word search](/puzzles/word-search/human-body-word-search/).** Twenty body-part words, from eyebrow to skeleton, in four directions. Ages 9 to 12.
14. **[Elephant dot to dot](/puzzles/dot-to-dot/elephant-dot-to-dot-hard/).** Fifty-seven dots, hard level, for students who find the easy ones too quick.

## Early finishers in middle school

**Middle schoolers want a real challenge, not a coloring sheet.** Give them puzzles adults enjoy.

15. **[Easy sudoku](/puzzles/sudoku/easy-sudoku/).** Forty 9 × 9 puzzles with 38 clues each, solvable with singles only (squares where only one number fits). A good first full-size sudoku.
16. **[Medium sudoku](/puzzles/sudoku/medium-sudoku/).** Twenty puzzles with about 32 clues, two per page in large print, still solvable without guessing.
17. **[Science word search](/puzzles/word-search/science-word-search/).** Twenty science words, from atom to photosynthesis, in a 20 × 20 grid in all eight directions. Ages 12 and up.
18. **[Countries word search](/puzzles/word-search/countries-word-search/).** Twenty countries in a 20 × 20 grid, all eight directions. Pair it with a map for a geography class.
19. **[Hard maze](/puzzles/mazes/hard-maze/).** A 38 × 38 maze with thin walls and deep dead ends; expect fifteen minutes or more. Our guide on [how to solve a maze](/blog/how-to-solve-a-maze/) explains the tricks that work.
20. **[Steam locomotive dot to dot](/puzzles/dot-to-dot/steam-locomotive-dot-to-dot-expert/).** One hundred seventy-two dots for the patient finisher.

For two students who finish at the same time, a pencil game is a quiet alternative: [dots and boxes](/rules/dots-and-boxes/), [Sim](/rules/sim/) (six dots and two colors) or [Sprouts](/rules/sprouts/), invented by the mathematicians John Conway and Michael Paterson. All three need only paper and a pencil.

## Setting up an early finisher station

**A station turns "what do I do now?" into a routine.** It takes an afternoon to set up and a few minutes a week to refill.

- **One bin or folder per level.** Even in a single grade, an easier and a harder folder let every student find something that fits.
- **Copies, not originals.** Print a master of each puzzle and photocopy a small stack. For mazes and word searches, a page in a sheet protector with a dry-erase marker can be reused all year.
- **Answer keys apart.** Keep the answer pages of every PDF (page 2, or the back pages of the sudoku books) in a separate binder near your desk. Students check their own work there and put the key back.
- **Three rules on a card**: work quietly, finish what you start, check your answers before you take another page.
- **A finished tray.** Completed puzzles go in a tray, so you can see at a glance who used the station and what they chose.
- **A choice board.** Let students pick, or let the picker below decide for them.

## Fun Friday and brain break versions

**The same printables work for the whole class.** On a Fun Friday, set out the puzzles as stations and let students rotate every ten minutes, or hold a class sudoku race on the projector.

- **Classroom bingo.** Our [classroom bingo cards](/bingo/classroom-bingo/) use 36 school words, from pencil to principal, with calling cards to cut out; 20 different cards cover most classes. The [bingo patterns guide](/blog/bingo-patterns/) suggests quick patterns.
- **A tic-tac-toe tournament.** Pairs, a bracket on the board, and our guide on [how to win tic-tac-toe](/blog/how-to-win-tic-tac-toe/) for the winners to study.
- **Games with no materials at all.** For a two-minute brain break between lessons, our list of [games to play without anything](/blog/games-to-play-without-anything/) has 20 ideas, from Simon Says to Twenty Questions.
