Chess and checkers share a board, a table and often a box, so the question comes up in every family: which one should we play? The short answer is that chess is harder and older, checkers is quicker to learn and quicker to play, and neither is a "kids' version" of the other. Here is the side-by-side comparison, the numbers behind "harder", the history, and a quiz that picks one for your table.

## Chess vs checkers at a glance

| | Checkers (English draughts) | Chess |
|---|---|---|
| Board | 8×8, only the 32 dark squares used | 8×8, all 64 squares used |
| Pieces | 12 each, 24 in all | 16 each, 32 in all |
| Kinds of piece | 2: men and kings | 6: king, queen, rook, bishop, knight, pawn |
| Who moves first | Dark pieces (red or black) | White |
| Capturing | Jump over a piece; **compulsory** | Move onto its square; optional |
| How you win | Opponent has no legal move | Checkmate |
| No legal move | A loss | A draw (stalemate) if not in check |
| Promotion | A man reaching the far row becomes a king | A pawn becomes a queen, rook, bishop or knight |
| Game length | 10–30 minutes | 20–90 minutes |
| Age to learn | 6 and up | 6 and up |
| Solved? | Yes, in 2007: perfect play is a draw | No |
| Origins | Medieval Europe, from alquerque | Chaturanga, India, by the 6th century |

Rules come from the WCDF and FIDE rulebooks; lengths and ages from our [checkers rules](/rules/checkers/) and [chess rules](/rules/chess/) pages.

## Same board, very different games

**Both games use the same 8×8 board, set the same way round.** The checkers rules ask for a dark square in each player's near left corner; the chess rules ask for a light square in the near right corner. On a standard board those are the same thing, so you never need to turn the board when you switch games.

<Figure src="/img/blog/chess-vs-checkers/same-board.svg" alt="Two boards side by side: checkers with 12 red and 12 white pieces on the dark squares of the first three rows, chess with 16 pieces per side on the first two rows" caption="The starting positions. Checkers fills the dark squares of three rows per side; chess fills the first two rows." width={1100} height={590} />

After that, almost everything differs:

- **Movement.** In checkers every piece moves the same way, one square diagonally, forwards only until it is crowned. In chess each of the six pieces has its own move, and there are three special moves to learn: castling, en passant and promotion.
- **Captures.** A checkers capture is a jump over the enemy piece, and it is compulsory: if you can jump, you must. In chess you capture by moving onto the enemy piece's square, and you never have to.
- **The goal.** Checkers is about material and mobility: you win when your opponent cannot move, usually because their pieces are gone. Chess is about one piece: checkmate the king and the game ends, whatever else is on the board.
- **Running out of moves.** In checkers that is a loss. In chess, a player who is not in check and has no legal move is stalemated, and the game is a draw.

The compulsory capture is what gives checkers its character. Because you can force your opponent's reply, the game is full of shots: give one piece, take two. Our guide on [how to win at checkers](/blog/how-to-win-at-checkers/) shows them on diagrams.

## Which is harder? Complexity and the solved-game question

**Chess is harder by every measure that can be counted.** It has more kinds of piece, more rules, more choices per move and vastly more positions.

The gap widens as the game goes on. The Chinook team put the number of checkers positions at about 5 × 10<sup>20</sup>. For chess, John Tromp's 2021 estimate is about 4.6 × 10<sup>44</sup> legal positions, a number with 24 more digits.

That size is why only checkers is solved. In 2007, Jonathan Schaeffer's team at the University of Alberta published the proof in *Science*: **with perfect play from both sides, checkers is a draw**. Chess is not solved. Computer "tablebases" give the perfect result for every chess position with up to seven pieces, the last of them completed in 2018, and work on eight-piece endings is still under way.

Solved does not mean easy. The *Science* paper calls checkers the most challenging popular game solved up to then, roughly a million times as complex as [Connect Four](/rules/connect-four/), and no human can play it perfectly. Computers also beat the human champions of checkers first: Chinook won the Man-Machine World Championship in 1994, three years before IBM's Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov at chess in 1997.

<Callout type="note" title="Harder to learn vs harder to master">
Chess is harder to learn: you need a page for how the pieces move before you play at all. Checkers is easy to learn and still hard to master; at top level, tournaments draw the first three moves at random from a list of accepted openings to cut down on draws.
</Callout>

## Which is older?

**Chess, by several centuries.** Its earliest known ancestor is chaturanga, which flourished in India by the 6th century and already had the two features of all later chess games: pieces with different powers, and a game decided by the fate of the king. The modern queen and bishop moves appeared in Spain around 1475, which is when chess took close to its present form.

Checkers is younger as a chessboard game. Wikipedia traces it to alquerque, a capturing game on a 5×5 grid of lines that an Arab scholar mentioned in the 10th century and that the Moors brought to Spain. Moved onto a chequered board with diagonal moves, the European game was being played around 1100, probably in the south of France. In 1243 it was called *fierges*, the name of the chess queen at the time.

So checkers borrowed its board from chess, and even its first name. The popular claim that checkers goes back to ancient Egypt or Ur does not hold up well: Wikipedia notes that the game found at Ur was a race game closer to backgammon, and that the Egyptian evidence is doubtful.

If you are curious about the other branch of the family, the prevailing view is that chaturanga is also the ancestor of xiangqi; our [printable Chinese chess set](/board-games/printable-chinese-chess/) has the board and pieces.

## Which is better for kids and beginners?

Both of our rules pages put the starting age at 6, so the choice depends more on the child and the time you have than on age.

**Checkers suits a first game.** There are two kinds of piece, one way to move and one way to capture, and a game fits into 10 to 30 minutes. The compulsory jump also teaches a useful habit early: before you move, look at what your opponent will be forced to do.

**Chess suits a player who enjoys rules.** Learning six moves, castling, en passant and check takes a few sessions, and games run longer, from 20 minutes to well over an hour. In return there is far more variety, and a huge body of books, puzzles and clubs to grow into.

<Callout type="tip" title="One route that works">
Start with checkers on the dark squares. When the rules feel easy, keep the same board and add chess one piece at a time: pawns first, then rooks, bishops, the queen and the knights, with the king last.
</Callout>
