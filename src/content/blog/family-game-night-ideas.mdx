## How do you plan a family game night in 15 minutes?

**Start from the youngest player and the clock**, not from the game. A four-year-old and a bedtime at eight rule out a 30-minute storytelling game; a table of teenagers can take one on easily. Then build a three-part evening:

1. **An opener (5–15 minutes)** that everyone already knows or learns in a minute: Spot It!, Spoons or Crazy Eights.
2. **A main game (20–30 minutes)** chosen for the age of the group: Timeline, Love Letter, Dixit or Show & Tile.
3. **A closer (5 minutes)** with no winner or a silly one: Heads, Bodies and Legs, or one more round of the opener.

Print and cut before dinner, put a pencil and a score pad on the table, and let the person who lost the last game choose the next one. That one rule ends most arguments before they start.

## Which games work for mixed ages, from 4 to 94?

The best mixed-age games reward something other than experience: a quick eye, luck, or noise.

**Spot It!** is the standout. Wikipedia describes the full deck as 55 cards with eight symbols each, and any two cards share exactly one symbol; the first to name it wins the card. Speed counts for more than experience, so a sharp-eyed child can beat the adults, which is the whole charm. Asmodee's free demo is a reduced deck, enough for a first evening.

<ItemCallout href="/card-games/spot-it-dobble-print-and-play-demo/" note="Free demo kit from Asmodee. Print in colour on card stock; a free My asmodee account is needed to download." />

**Spoons** needs a deck and one spoon fewer than the players. Bicycle puts it at ages 4 and up: pass cards round, collect four of a kind, grab a spoon, and the slowest hand misses out. **Crazy Eights** is the gentler card option: match the top card by suit or rank, eights are wild, and the first to empty their hand wins. **Snakes and Ladders**, sold in the US as Chutes and Ladders, is pure luck, which makes it fair for a preschooler; our rules page has a free printable board.

**Timeline** brings in the grandparents. You do not need to know dates, only whether an invention came before or after the cards already on the table.

## What are good quick games for a school night?

On a weeknight you want games that take less time to play than to explain. These four take 20 minutes or less.

- **Scuttle!** is a pirate card game by Peter C. Hayward: draw a card or play one as treasure or as an action, and be first to 21 doubloons. Ages 6 and up.
- **Meow!**, by the same designer, is a game of secret rules inspired by Mao. Each player follows rules only the others can see, and you win the moment you break none. It seats up to 12.
- **Love Letter** is Seiji Kanai's tiny game of guessing and bluffing for two to four: draw a card, play one, and knock rivals out of the round. Play to an agreed number of tokens.
- **Village Pillage** has everyone play one card against each neighbour at the same time, farm turnips and steal them back. First to three relics wins.

## Which drawing and party games suit families?

Drawing games level the field: the worst artist at the table is often the funniest.

**Heads, Bodies and Legs** is the fold-and-pass game the Surrealists called exquisite corpse; Wikipedia notes it is close to the old parlour game Consequences, and calls the drawn version picture consequences. One player draws a head, folds it over, and passes it on for a body, then legs. Nobody wins and everyone laughs.

<ItemCallout href="/party-games/printable-heads-bodies-and-legs-game/" note="A drawing sheet with three boxes and a cover with flaps. Black and white, three Letter pages." />

**Quick on the Draw** is a team drawing game in the style of Pictionary, the game Robert Angel first published in 1985. Our printable gives four category cards; you bring the word list and a one-minute timer.

<Timer seconds={60} title="One-minute drawing timer" />

**Dixit** is the storytelling game by Jean-Louis Roubira, first published by Libellud in 2008. The storyteller gives a clue about a dreamlike picture, everyone else slips in a card that fits, and the table votes. A clue that is too obvious scores nothing, so even children learn to be cleverly vague. **Show & Tile** swaps pencils for tangram tiles: build a secret word in 90 seconds, then guess everyone else's.

## Which classic board and dice games can you print?

If your family prefers a board, you do not need to buy one. Our [printable boards](/board-games/printable-boards/) include Snakes and Ladders, [Pachisi](/board-games/printable-pachisi-board/) for two to four players and [Chinese Checkers](/board-games/printable-chinese-checkers/) for up to six.

For dice, **Yacht** is the public-domain game that Wikipedia dates to at least 1938, before Yahtzee: roll five dice up to three times and fill twelve categories. Our rules page has a free score sheet. For a big family gathering, [Bunco](/rules/bunco/) and [Left Right Center](/rules/left-right-center/) need only dice and seat up to 12.

## How do you run a theme night?

A theme gives the evening a reason to happen and makes printing part of the fun. Three that work with this list:

| Theme | Main game | Add-ons |
|---|---|---|
| Thanksgiving | [Thanksgiving bingo](/bingo/thanksgiving-bingo/), 20 cards | Spoons between courses, Timeline for the grandparents |
| Christmas | [Christmas bingo](/bingo/christmas-bingo/), 20 cards | [Christmas word search](/puzzles/word-search/christmas-word-search/) as a race, Heads, Bodies and Legs with festive monsters |
| Puzzle night | A maze race on [hard mazes](/puzzles/mazes/hard/) | Easy mazes for little ones, [sudoku](/puzzles/sudoku/) for teens |

Halloween works too: [Brains!](/card-games/brains/) is a zombie card game for two to five from age 6.

<Callout type="note" title="Snacks and seating">
Keep food in bowls away from the cards, or serve it between games. Seat a younger child next to the most patient adult, and let them hold the score pad: keeping score is a job, not a punishment.
</Callout>
