Easter scavenger hunt clues turn a basket search into a real adventure: every clue sends the hunters to the next hiding spot, and the last one leads to the basket. The trick is matching the clue to the hunter. Below are 40 Easter hunt clues, each with its answer: picture clues for toddlers, rhymes for kids, riddles for teenagers and cryptic clues for adults. At the end, you will find how to turn a printable maze or word search into a clue station.

## How an Easter clue hunt works (and how many clues you need)

**A clue hunt is a chain.** Clue 1 is handed over; it describes a hiding place, where clue 2 waits; and so on until the final clue points to the basket or the prize. Whether you call it an Easter scavenger hunt or an Easter egg hunt with clues, the setup is the same:

1. **Hide the prize first**, out of sight of the trail.
2. **Hide the clues in reverse order.** Put the last clue somewhere, then the clue that describes that spot somewhere else, and keep going until you hold clue 1.
3. **Number the list on your phone** as you hide, so you can rescue a stuck hunter.
4. **Put a few eggs or treats at each stop** if you like; the clue chain works without them.

The hidden eggs themselves are an old idea. Wikipedia traces the Easter Bunny to the "Easter Hare" of German Lutherans, who brought colored eggs to children who had been good; Protestant German immigrants in the Pennsylvania Dutch area carried the custom to America in the 18th century. Today's egg hunt uses hard-boiled, chocolate or plastic eggs, hidden indoors or out.

How many clues? It depends on age and attention:

| Hunters | Clues | Clue style | Tip |
|---|---|---|---|
| Toddlers (2–4) | 5–6 | Photo or drawing of the spot | Keep it to one or two rooms |
| Kids (5–9) | 8–12 | Two-line rhymes, read aloud if needed | Pitch it at the youngest reader |
| Teens | 10–12 | Riddles that never name the spot | Hide clues inside things, add a timer |
| Adults | 8–10 | Cryptic crossword-style clues | Mix with a puzzle station or two |

## Picture clues for toddlers and preschoolers

**Children who cannot read follow pictures.** Take a photo of each hiding place on your phone and print it small, or draw a quick sketch on a card. Read the line aloud as you hand it over, and keep the whole trail within one or two rooms.

| # | Picture on the card | Say aloud | Hiding spot |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The bathtub | "Where does your rubber duck swim?" | Bathtub |
| 2 | The fridge | "Where do we keep the milk?" | Fridge (bottom shelf) |
| 3 | Their bed | "Where do you sleep at night?" | On the bed |
| 4 | Their shoes | "Where are your shoes?" | Shoe rack or shoe basket |
| 5 | The toy box | "Where do your toys go?" | Toy box |
| 6 | The couch | "Where do we sit to watch TV?" | Couch |
| 7 | The doormat | "Where do we wipe our feet?" | Doormat |
| 8 | The bookshelf | "Where are your books?" | Bookshelf (low shelf) |
| 9 | The laundry basket | "Where do dirty socks go?" | Laundry basket |
| 10 | Their teddy bear | "Who do you hug at bedtime?" | Next to their teddy |

Fill toddler eggs with stickers or large toys rather than small candy, and keep every clue at their eye level.

## Rhyming Easter clues for kids

**For children from about 5 to 9, a two-line rhyme is the classic Easter hunt clue.** The rhyme lands on the place, not on the prize. Read each one aloud before you hide it: if you hesitate, so will they.

1. I'm cold inside but never sad; I keep the milk from going bad. *(fridge)*
2. Bubbles, splashes, a rubber duck: look in the tub and try your luck! *(bathtub)*
3. I have four legs but cannot walk; we eat our dinner here and talk. *(kitchen table)*
4. I hold your stories, short and tall, standing in rows against the wall. *(bookshelf)*
5. Sneakers, sandals, boots for rain: look where they wait to go out again. *(shoe rack)*
6. Fluffy and soft, I hold your head: look under me on top of your bed. *(pillow)*
7. I swish and I swirl and I wash all your clothes; open my door and see where the hunt goes. *(washing machine)*
8. Snuggle up for a movie night: check under the cushions, left and right. *(couch)*
9. Dirty socks and muddy jeans wait in me for the washing machine. *(laundry basket)*
10. Blocks and cars and dolls go here at the end of the day: lift my lid to find your way. *(toy box)*

Kids old enough to read alone can take turns reading the clue out loud, which stops the fastest reader from running off with the hunt.

## Harder Easter riddles for teenagers

**Teenagers want a riddle, not a rhyme.** These never name the place; the hunter has to work it out. Hide the next clue inside the object where you can (in a towel's fold, behind the TV), and add a timer to make it a race.

1. I have keys but open no locks, a space but no room, and you can enter but never go inside. *(computer keyboard)*
2. I say "welcome" to everyone, yet everyone walks all over me. *(doormat)*
3. I'm full of holes, yet I still hold water. *(kitchen sponge)*
4. The more I dry, the wetter I get. *(bath towel)*
5. I have a head and a foot but no body, and you spend about a third of your life with me. *(bed)*
6. I have a screen but I'm not a window, and I only do what the remote tells me. *(TV)*
7. I have a ring but no finger, I live by the front door, and I only speak when you press my button. *(doorbell)*
8. I have a door and a light that switches off when you close me; inside it is always cold, and the milk lives here. *(fridge)*
9. I have cycles but no pedals, I fill with water, I spin without getting dizzy, and I am known to eat socks. *(washing machine)*
10. We stand side by side with spines but no bones and leaves but no branches: find where we live. *(bookshelf)*

If your home has two of something (two TVs, two bookshelves), add one word to the riddle, such as "upstairs", so the answer stays unambiguous.

## Cryptic Easter egg hunt clues for adults

**Adults enjoy a clue they have to crack.** These are written like cryptic crossword clues: one end of the clue is a plain definition of the hiding place, the rest is wordplay that builds the same word, and the number in brackets is its length. Give the group one worked example first.

1. Place to sit hidden in also famous (4). *(SOFA: hidden in "al**so fa**mous")*
2. Skin damaged where the dishes get washed (4). *(SINK: SKIN rearranged)*
3. Female on a mountain crest keeps things cold (6). *(FRIDGE: F + RIDGE)*
4. Near the top of trees, it holds coats (6). *(CLOSET: CLOSE + T)*
5. Club, hospital and barrel for a soak (7). *(BATHTUB: BAT + H + TUB)*
6. Pull the Spanish dryer (5). *(TOWEL: TOW + EL, Spanish for "the")*
7. Cast off a garden store (4). *(SHED: two meanings)*
8. Artist's place for socks (6). *(DRAWER: one who draws, and the sock drawer)*
9. Skillet attempt makes a food store (6). *(PANTRY: PAN + TRY)*
10. Car's home: Georgia, then temper (6). *(GARAGE: GA + RAGE)*

Pair adults up: one solver and one runner keeps everyone involved. For a final challenge, put the last clue in a padlocked box and hide the code in the earlier clues (one digit per clue), the way escape room games do; our guide to [escape room board games](/blog/escape-room-board-games/) has more ideas for locks and codes.

## Indoor vs outdoor hiding spots

**Indoors, the hunt is weatherproof; outdoors, there is more room to run.** Pick the list that fits the forecast, and walk the trail once before the hunters do.

| Indoor spots | Outdoor spots | Keep out of the hunt |
|---|---|---|
| Fridge (bottom shelf) | Playhouse or fort | Oven, stove and microwave |
| Bathtub | Under the doormat | Fireplace and candles |
| Bookshelf | Flowerpot or planter | Trash and recycling bins |
| Under a pillow | Garden hose or outdoor faucet | Anything that needs a ladder |
| Shoe rack | Swing, slide or sandbox | Garage tools and chemicals |
| Washing machine | Bird feeder | Busy road edges |
| Laundry basket | Porch steps | Ponds and pools |
| Couch cushions | Bench or patio chair | Plants children should not touch |

Outdoor clues blow away: put each in a zip bag or a plastic egg. If you hide real hard-boiled eggs, the FDA's advice is simple: hard-boil them before coloring, and do not eat them after they have been hidden or played with.

## Turn a puzzle into a clue: maze and word search stations

**A puzzle station makes the hunters earn the next location.** It also slows a fast group down so younger siblings can catch up. Four free printables work well as stations.

**Easter maze (ages 6 to 9).** Before the hunt, open the solution on page 2 of our [Easter maze](/puzzles/mazes/easter-maze/), then write the letters of the next hiding place, one per corridor, along the correct path on the puzzle page. Add random letters in the dead ends. Only the hunter who finds the way out can read the word.

<ItemCallout href="/puzzles/mazes/easter-egg-maze/" note="An easy egg-shaped maze with thick walls for ages 3 to 6. For toddlers, draw a small picture of the next hiding place at the exit instead of letters." />

**Easter word search.** Our [Easter word search](/puzzles/word-search/easter-word-search/) hides 15 words, including carrot, basket, bonnet and nest. Put a star next to one word in the list: CARROT sends them to the vegetable drawer of the fridge, BASKET to the Easter basket, BONNET to the hat shelf. The hunters have to find the starred word in the grid before they move on.

**Dot to dot.** The finished picture is the clue. Our [duck dot to dot](/puzzles/dot-to-dot/duck-dot-to-dot/) (30 dots) reveals a duck: the next clue waits by the rubber duck in the bathtub. The [alphabet rabbit dot to dot](/puzzles/dot-to-dot/abc-rabbit-dot-to-dot/) (A to Z) reveals a bunny: look next to a stuffed toy rabbit.
