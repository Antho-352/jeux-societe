## Which guessing and word games need no equipment at all?

**Word games are the easiest games to play without anything**: the only material is language. All five work at a table, on a walk or in a car.

### 1. Twenty Questions

The answerer thinks of something. The others take turns asking questions answered only "yes" or "no", and lying is not allowed. Whoever guesses it answers next; if twenty questions pass without a correct guess, the answerer wins and goes again. Start broad ("Is it alive?") and narrow down.

**Variant for families:** "animal, vegetable or mineral", where the answerer names the kingdom first. For friends, swap the twenty questions for one minute on the clock.

<Timer seconds={60} title="One-minute guessing round" />

### 2. Contact

One player, the defender, picks a secret word and says its first letter, say "C". A guesser gives a clue for any word starting with C ("a small dog"), and another guesser who thinks they know it shouts "Contact!". Unless the defender names a matching word first ("it's not chihuahua"), the two guessers count down from three and say their word together. If they match, the defender reveals the next letter, and every new clue must start with all the letters shown so far. The round ends when two guessers make contact on the secret word itself.

**Tip for friends:** oblique clues win. "Mozart's instrument" beats "a piano" because the defender must decode it before your partner does.

### 3. Ghost

Players take turns adding one letter to a growing fragment, and every fragment must be the start of a real word. Whoever completes a real word loses the round; most groups count only words of three or four letters or more. Instead of adding a letter, you may challenge the previous player; if they cannot name a real word that starts with the fragment, they lose the round, otherwise you do. Each lost round earns a letter of G-H-O-S-T, and spelling the whole word knocks you out.

**Variant for couples:** Superghost, where you may add a letter at the front or the end of the fragment.

### 4. Botticelli

The chooser thinks of a famous person, at least as famous as the painter Botticelli, and announces the initial of their surname. Guessers then take turns thinking of someone with that initial and asking an indirect question: "Are you bald?" If the chooser names someone who fits ("No, I am not Yul Brynner"), play moves on; if stumped, the chooser must answer one direct yes-or-no question about the mystery person. You win by asking "Are you…?" with the right name.

**Variant for families:** allow fictional characters, or people everyone at the table knows, so children can play with adults.

### 5. Word chain

Agree on a category, such as food or animals. Each player names a word that starts with the last letter of the previous word: soup, peas, sugar, rice. You have about five seconds, words may not repeat, and whoever hesitates or repeats is out. With countries, cities and rivers, the game is called Geography.

**Variant for families:** drop the category for younger children; any real word goes.

## Which acting and party games need no props?

These four need a room and some nerve. Charades works from preschool up; the other three reward a straight face.

### 6. Charades

Split into two teams. A player from one team gets a film, book or phrase whispered by the other team, then mimes it to their own team without speaking. Conventional gestures help: hold up fingers for the number of words, lay fingers on the inside of your elbow for the number of syllables, and tug your earlobe for "sounds like". Teams alternate, and the faster team wins the round.

**Variant for families:** let children mime animals or jobs instead of titles. If you have a pencil, our [Quick on the Draw scorecards](/party-games/printable-quick-on-the-draw/) turn it into a drawing game.

### 7. Wink Murder

One player, the detective, leaves the room, and the rest silently choose a murderer by pointing. The detective returns to the middle of the circle. The murderer kills by winking at someone, who dies theatrically after a short delay. The detective has three guesses to name the murderer, and a wrong accusation "kills" the innocent player named. The murderer wins by killing as many players as possible before being caught.

**Variant for adults:** the "deadly handshake": everyone mingles, and the murderer kills with a secret signal in a handshake.

### 8. Mafia, without cards

Mafia needs at least six players plus a narrator who does not play. Everyone closes their eyes and the narrator taps the mafia on the shoulder. Andrew Plotkin, who gave the game its werewolf theme, recommends two mafia; Dmitry Davidoff's original rules use a third of the players. The game alternates night and day. At night, the narrator asks the mafia to open their eyes, recognise each other and silently point at a victim. At day, everyone opens their eyes, the narrator announces who was "killed", and the town debates and votes to eliminate one suspect. The town wins when every mafioso is out; the mafia win when they equal the number of townspeople left.

**Variant for friends:** add a detective role. Each night the narrator taps one player, the detective points at a suspect, and the narrator nods or shakes their head. If you have a printer and want proper role cards, [Secret Hitler](/party-games/secret-hitler/) and [Two Rooms and a Boom](/party-games/two-rooms-and-a-boom/) are free hidden-role games for bigger groups.

<Callout type="tip" title="Narrator's trick">
Ask everyone to tap their knees softly during the night phase. The noise hides the rustle of the mafia pointing, so nobody can guess who moved.
</Callout>

### 9. Never Have I Ever

Everyone holds up ten fingers. In turn, each player names something true they have never done: "Never have I ever been on a cruise." Every player who has done it puts down a finger, and the first with no fingers left loses.

**Variant for friends:** if only one person has done it, they tell the story. Our [Never Have I Ever page](/rules/never-have-i-ever/) covers more house rules, including the drinking version for adults 21+.

## What games can two people play with nothing?

Couples, a parent and child in a waiting room, two friends on a drive: these three are built for two, and Twenty Questions and Ghost work just as well.

### 10. Rock paper scissors

On "rock, paper, scissors, shoot", both players show a fist (rock), a flat hand (paper) or two fingers (scissors). Rock blunts scissors, scissors cut paper, paper wraps rock; the same sign is a tie. Play best of five to settle who does the dishes.

**Variant for kids:** play it with the whole body, crouching for rock, arms wide for paper and legs apart for scissors.

### 11. Chopsticks

Each player starts with one finger raised on each hand. On your turn, either tap one of your hands against one of your opponent's, adding your fingers to theirs (two fingers tapped by one become three), or tap your own hands together to share your fingers differently between them. A hand that reaches five or more is dead and goes behind your back. Knock out both of your opponent's hands to win.

**Variant:** in the "roll-over" version a hand dies only on exactly five; three plus three becomes one. Games last longer and need more counting.

### 12. Would You Rather

Ask a dilemma: "Would you rather fly or be invisible?" The other player must choose; "neither" and "both" are not allowed. Then each defends their choice. There is no winner, which is why it suits a date or a long dinner.

**Variant for couples:** answer for your partner before they answer for themselves, and score a point for each correct prediction.

## Which no-equipment games work for families with young kids?

**For children under seven, pick games with short turns and no reading.** All three are classic car games.

### 13. I Spy

The spy picks an object everyone can see and says, "I spy with my little eye something beginning with…" and names its first letter. The others guess until someone gets it and becomes the next spy. The spy may not change the object once chosen.

**Variant for toddlers:** give a colour instead of a letter: "I spy something blue." In a car, agree that the object must stay in view.

### 14. Simon Says

One player is Simon and gives orders: "Simon says touch your nose." Players must obey only orders that start with "Simon says"; anyone who follows an order without it, or fails to follow one with it, is out. The last player standing becomes Simon.

**Variant:** "Do this, do that". Copy the leader's action when they say "do this", and stay still when they say "do that".

### 15. I Packed My Bag

The first player says, "I packed my bag and in it I put a toothbrush." The next repeats the whole list and adds one item: "a toothbrush and a tent". Anyone who forgets an item or cannot add one is out, and the last player with a complete list wins.

**Variant for readers:** go through the alphabet (an apple, a banana, a comb), which makes the list easier to remember.

<Callout type="note" title="Mixed ages at the same table">
Give younger children a role instead of a handicap: Simon, the Twenty Questions answerer or the I Spy spy. Answering is easier than guessing. Our [Thanksgiving games for family](/blog/thanksgiving-games-for-family/) has more ideas for big gatherings.
</Callout>

## What can you play if you find a pen and paper?

One pencil and the back of a receipt open up the [pen-and-paper games](/rules/pen-and-paper/). All five below have a full rules page on this site, and three have a printable sheet.

### 16. Dots and Boxes

Draw a grid of dots. Players take turns joining two neighbouring dots with a line. Whoever draws the fourth side of a box writes their initial in it and must play again. When the grid is full, the most boxes wins. It looks like luck at first; once you learn about chains of boxes, the game becomes a real duel.

<ItemCallout href="/rules/dots-and-boxes/" note="Full rules, the chain and double-cross strategy, and a printable sheet of four 6×6 dot grids." />

### 17. Sprouts

Draw two or three spots. On your turn, join two spots with a line (or loop a spot back to itself) and add a new spot on that line. Lines may never cross, and no spot may have more than three lines. The player who makes the last possible move wins.

<ItemCallout href="/rules/sprouts/" note="The three line rules, why every game must end, and who wins with perfect play." />

### 18. Sim

Draw six dots in a hexagon; fifteen lines can join them in pairs. Players take turns drawing one line each in their own colour, and the first to complete a triangle in their own colour loses. A game takes about two minutes, and the [Sim rules](/rules/sim/) explain why it can never end in a draw.

### 19. Hangman

One player thinks of a word and draws a dash for each letter. The others guess letters one at a time; a right letter is written on its dash, a wrong one adds a part to the gallows drawing. The guessers win if they find the word before the drawing is finished. In class, many teachers build a snowman or cross apples off a tree instead of drawing a gallows.

<ItemCallout href="/rules/hangman/" note="Rules, kinder alternatives to the gallows, and a printable sheet with six games on one page." />

### 20. Heads, Bodies and Legs

Fold a sheet into three. The first player draws a head, folds it out of sight with only the neck lines showing, and passes it on; the next draws a body, the last the legs. Unfold and laugh. Nobody wins, which makes it a good closer for a family evening.

<ItemCallout href="/party-games/printable-heads-bodies-and-legs-game/" note="A ready-made drawing sheet with three boxes and a cover with flaps. Three black-and-white Letter pages." />

Two more pencil classics need only a grid: [tic-tac-toe](/rules/tic-tac-toe/) for a minute, and [Battleship](/rules/battleship/) for a longer duel with two sheets kept out of sight.
