People who love Clue rarely love the dice rolls between rooms. What they love is the moment the notes add up and they can name the culprit. The ten games below keep that moment and change the rest: some are closer whodunits, some let players lie, and six have free files you can print tonight. Every player count, age and time comes from BoardGameGeek or the publisher, and a quiz at the end matches a game to your group.

## What makes Clue fun? Deduction in a nutshell

**Clue is a game of elimination.** At the start, one suspect card, one weapon card and one room card go into a secret envelope, and the rest are dealt out. You walk into a room and make a suggestion ("Professor Plum, in the dining room, with the candlestick"); the next player who holds one of those cards must show you one. Each card you see is one more possibility crossed off your notes. With six suspects, six weapons and nine rooms, Wikipedia counts 324 possible solutions.

The game is older than most people think. Anthony E. Pratt, a musician from Birmingham, England, devised it during the Second World War and applied for a patent in 1944 for a game then called Murder. Waddingtons launched it as Cluedo in 1949, and Parker Brothers published it in the United States the same year as Clue. Hasbro owns it today.

**So a game "like Clue" needs a hidden answer and a fair way to narrow it down.** The games below do it with questions, with cards you are forced to reveal, or with people who might be lying to you.

## Which games are most like Clue for families?

These three keep Clue's core: one card is hidden, and you find it by asking questions.

**Mystery of the Abbey** is the closest. BoardGameGeek calls Bruno Faidutti and Serge Laget's game (1995, published in English by Days of Wonder) "a whodunit deduction game in the spirit of Clue". A monk has been murdered, one monk card is hidden, and the rest are dealt out. Each monk is a mix of traits: order, title, fat or thin, hooded or bald, bearded or clean-shaven. You move up to two spaces, then ask a player in the same room something like "how many fat monks do you have?". They answer truthfully or take a vow of silence. Every four turns, Mass calls everyone back to the Sanctuary and a growing number of cards pass to the left. Unlike Clue, a correct accusation earns points rather than the game, and naming single traits scores too. It plays three to six, from age 8, in 60 to 90 minutes.

**Cryptid** (Hal Duncan and Ruth Veevers, Osprey Games, 2018) turns the hidden card into a hidden place. A creature is hiding somewhere on a modular map, and each player holds one piece of evidence about where. On your turn you try to learn more from your opponents, but you cannot find the beast without giving away some of what you know. The publisher calls it "honest misdirection". Three to five players, from age 10, with hundreds of set-ups across two difficulty levels.

**Sherlock 13** (Hope S. Hwang, 2013, first published as Holmes 13) is Clue in 15 minutes. Thirteen character cards each carry two or three traits; one is set aside as the disguise of the thief Arsène Lupin, and the rest are dealt out. You ask the table "who has at least one of this trait?" or one player "how many do you have?", and count until only one card fits. Accuse wrongly and you are out. It plays two to four.

## Which games add bluffing and hidden identities?

**In Clue every answer is true. In these three, someone at the table wants you to get it wrong.**

**Deception: Murder in Hong Kong** (Tobey Ho, 2014) is the closest in theme. The Forensic Scientist knows the solution but may only point to it with scene tiles, never with words or gestures. One of the investigators is secretly the Murderer, who chose the key evidence and the means of murder, and an Accomplice may help them. The investigators win by naming both. It handles four to twelve players in about 20 minutes, from age 14.

**Love Letter** (Seiji Kanai, 2012) is deduction in your pocket: 16 cards, one card in hand, and each turn you draw one and play one. Card effects let you guess a rival's card, peek at a hand or compare hands, so you count what has been played and read faces. It plays two to four, from age 10, and Asmodee gives away the cards and rules free.

<ItemCallout href="/card-games/love-letter-print-and-play/" note="Asmodee's free Love Letter print and play: the cards and rules in one file. A free My asmodee account unlocks it." />

**Secret Hitler** (Mike Boxleiter, Tommy Maranges and Max Temkin, 2016) is the big-group pick. Players are secretly liberals or fascists, and one fascist is Hitler. The fascists know each other; the liberals know nothing but have the majority. Each round the table elects a President and a Chancellor, who pass a law from a random deck, and every fascist law makes you wonder who chose it. Five to ten players, 45 minutes, ages 13 and up. The publisher released the full game under a Creative Commons licence, and [our Secret Hitler page](/party-games/secret-hitler/) hosts the official print-and-play.

## Which quick deduction games can you print free?

**Three games from Jellybean Games bring Clue's "who is it?" to a party table in 20 minutes or less.** The publisher allows rehosting with credit, so the files are on our pages.

**Dracula's Feast** (Peter C. Hayward, art by Tania Walker, 2017) seats four to eight. Everyone is a secret guest at Dracula's dinner, each with a special ability, and one guest lies face down as the Mystery Guest. On your turn you ask a player "are you the Werewolf?" (they answer with a secret Yes or No card), ask someone to dance (you secretly show each other your cards), or accuse. To win you must name every guest at once, and only if every answer is Yes. It takes 10 to 15 minutes, from age 10.

<ItemCallout href="/card-games/dracula-s-feast/" note="The first edition with 13 guests: guest and accusation cards, card backs and the rulebook. Print on card stock and sleeve." />

**Night of the Mummy** (2022, art by Michael Dashow) is the sequel, set at a museum's masquerade ball. The new idea: some guests hide their face when they dance, showing only the back of their card, so a dance no longer guarantees an answer. It plays four to eight in 15 to 20 minutes, and can be mixed with Dracula's Feast: New Blood.

<ItemCallout href="/card-games/night-of-the-mummy/" note="10 guests, accusation and whisper cards on 9 full-colour Letter sheets, plus the 16-page rulebook." />

**The Lady and the Tiger** (2017) is five games in eighteen cards. In Doors, the two-player game, one player holds a secret identity, such as Red Lady, and collects cards; the other watches what they take and guesses their colour, their role or both. A wrong guess costs you. Add 25 gems or tokens in five colours.

<ItemCallout href="/card-games/the-lady-and-the-tiger/" note="Five micro-games for 1 to 6 players, including Doors (two-player deduction) and Traps (bluffing for up to six)." />

<Callout type="note" title="Something different: the murder before the mystery">
[Kill Doctor Lucky](/board-games/kill-doctor-lucky/) is not a deduction game, so it is not one of the ten. James Ernest's 1996 Cheapass board game is a "pre-mystery": you sneak around a mansion of rooms trying to commit the murder Clue asks you to solve. It is free to download, if your group likes Clue's setting more than its notes.

## Is Battleship a good deduction game for kids?

**Yes: it is the simplest deduction game there is.** Each player hides five ships, from two to five squares long, on a grid. You call a square, hear "hit" or "miss", and use each answer to work out where the rest of the ship must be. BoardGameGeek notes it began as a public-domain pencil-and-paper game before Milton Bradley turned it into a board game in 1967, so a printed grid is the original way to play. It suits two players from age 8, the same age as Clue.
