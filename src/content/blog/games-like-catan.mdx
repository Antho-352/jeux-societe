Most lists of games like Catan stack twenty titles and stop. A better question is which part of Catan keeps you at the table: laying out the island, haggling for brick, watching your cities pay out, or just having a game that works for the people you play with. Below are twelve games sorted by that answer, with players, time and BoardGameGeek data for each, and free print-and-play demos for the ones you can try before buying. A short quiz at the end picks one for you.

## Why does Catan work, and what would you change?

**Catan combines four ideas in one box.** Klaus Teuber's game, first published by KOSMOS in 1995 as The Settlers of Catan, has you build a map of hexagonal tiles, roll two dice each turn to see which tiles produce resources, trade those resources with the other players, and spend them on roads, settlements and cities. The first to 10 points wins.

Each of those ideas is also what some players would change. **The dice decide your income**: on two dice, a 6 or an 8 comes up 5 times in 36 rolls, a 2 or a 12 only once, so a settlement on the wrong numbers can sit idle for half the game. **A 7 brings the robber**: the player who rolled it moves him onto a tile and steals a card from someone settled next to it. And the box seats only three or four; five and six players need the separate 5–6 Player Expansion.

## Which games are like Catan if you love building the map?

**Carcassonne** is the classic answer. Klaus-Jürgen Wrede's game, published by Hans im Glück in 2000 and winner of the Spiel des Jahres in 2001, starts with one tile on the table. Each turn you draw a square of French countryside, place it so roads meet roads and cities meet cities, and may put one of your followers on it. Finished roads, cities and monasteries score at once. Like Catan, the map is different every game; unlike Catan, there are no dice, and BoardGameGeek voters rate two players as its best count.

<ItemCallout href="/board-games/carcassonne-print-and-play-demo/" note="Asmodee's free English demo: land tiles to cut out and the demo rules. A free My asmodee account unlocks the download." />

**Kingdomino** is the 20-minute version of the same pleasure. Bruno Cathala's game (Blue Orange, 2016, Spiel des Jahres 2017) has you pick domino-shaped land tiles and add them to your own kingdom, next to a starting castle. Each territory scores its number of squares multiplied by its crowns, so a big lake with no crown is worth nothing. The tile you pick also sets the turn order for the next round: the best tile now usually means picking later next round.

If you already own Kingdomino, Blue Orange's free expansion adds resources to your lands, which you spend on buildings and courtiers. That is as close to Catan's "collect and spend" loop as the game gets.

<ItemCallout href="/board-games/kingdomino-the-court/" note="Kingdomino: The Court, 4 printable pages from Blue Orange. Needs Kingdomino or Queendomino." />

**Ticket to Ride** keeps Catan's road race on a fixed map of North America. Alan R. Moon's game (Days of Wonder, 2004, Spiel des Jahres 2004) gives you three choices per turn: draw train cards, claim a route, or take destination tickets that link two cities. The longest continuous route earns a bonus, much like Catan's Longest Road. It plays two to five and is lighter than Catan on BoardGameGeek.

## Which games are like Catan for trading and negotiation?

If the best moment in Catan is "I'll give you two sheep for one ore", **Bohnanza** is the game to buy. Uwe Rosenberg's bean-farming card game (AMIGO, 1997) has one twist: you may never rearrange your hand, and you must plant the front card each turn. Trading is how you get rid of the beans you cannot use, and every trade must involve the active player. It needs at least three players and comes into its own with four or five, which is the count BoardGameGeek voters rate best.

**Jaipur** is trading for two. Sébastien Pauchon's card game (2009) has a shared market of face-up cards: you take one card, take all the camels, or swap several of yours for cards in the market. Then you sell one type of goods at a time, and the tokens lose value as they go, so you weigh selling early against waiting for a bonus on a set of three, four or five.

<Callout type="tip" title="Trading on paper tonight">
For a free taste of a resource economy, [Village Pillage](/card-games/village-pillage/) from Jellybean Games has you farm turnips, protect them, steal them and trade them for relics in 10 to 15 minutes. It seats two to five.
</Callout>

## Which games let you grow an economy like Catan?

**Splendor** strips Catan down to its engine and removes the dice. Marc André's game (Space Cowboys, 2014) lets you take gem tokens or buy a card with them. Each card you buy gives a permanent gem discount on future cards, so your economy snowballs. Reach 15 prestige points before your rivals and you win. BoardGameGeek voters rate three as its best count.

**Stone Age** keeps the dice that pay out resources, but you choose where to roll. In Bernd Brunnhofer's game (Hans im Glück, 2008) you place tribe members in the forest, the quarry or the hunt, then roll one die per figure in each place and divide the total by the resource's value (2 for food, 3 for wood, up to 6 for gold) to see what they bring back. You must feed your tribe every round. At 2.45 it is the heaviest game on this list, a natural step up from Catan.

**Machi Koro** is the closest to Catan's production rule. In Masao Suganuma's game (2012) you roll one or two dice each turn; every building whose number comes up pays out, and some pay on your opponents' rolls too. You spend the coins on more buildings and race to finish your landmarks. Games take about 30 minutes.

**Citadels** builds a city with secret roles instead of dice. In Bruno Faidutti's card game, first published in 2000, everyone drafts a character each round (Assassin, Thief, King, Merchant and more), takes gold or district cards, and pays to build. The leader is always a target, which gives it the same table talk as a Catan trade. It seats two to eight.

<ItemCallout href="/card-games/citadels-print-and-play-demo/" note="Asmodee's free Citadels demo: character and district cards plus shortened rules. Any coins work as gold." />

For a full engine-builder that costs nothing, [Unfair](/card-games/unfair-base-game/) is the complete theme-park card game from 2am Gameworks for two to five players aged 14 and up. It is longer and meaner than Catan: about 25 minutes per player, with events you play against each other's parks.

## What is the best game like Catan for two players?

Catan itself does not play with two out of the box, so this is the most common reason people look for something else.

**Rivals for Catan** is the obvious pick because it is Catan. Klaus Teuber reworked his 1996 Catan Card Game into this two-player game (KOSMOS, 2010). Each player builds a small province of settlements, roads and cities from cards, and a die roll decides which of your regions produce each turn. The introductory game goes to 7 points, and BoardGameGeek's description puts it at 25 to 30 minutes for beginners; the three theme games add rules and take 45 to 60.

**7 Wonders Duel** is the other favourite. Antoine Bauza and Bruno Cathala's game (Repos Production, 2015) won BoardGameGeek's Golden Geek for best two-player game that year. You take cards from a shared layout, build resources, wonders, military and science, and can win by points, by marching on your rival's capital or by collecting science symbols. Resources you produce also make the same goods dearer for your opponent.

**Jaipur**, above, and **Carcassonne** both work very well for two as well. For more ideas, see our page of [print-and-play games for two players](/board-games/2-player/).

## Is there a cooperative game like Catan?

**Pandemic** is the one most groups move to after Catan. Matt Leacock's game (Z-Man Games, 2008) puts two to four players on the same team: each turn you get four actions to travel, treat disease cubes, build a research station or discover a cure, then the infection spreads. You all win by curing the diseases or all lose to outbreaks. It has the map and the card management of Catan, without anyone stealing your sheep.

You can try it free. Z-Man Games gives away the full print-and-play of Pandemic: Hot Zone – North America, a shorter version with three diseases and a smaller map, for two to four players from age 8.

## Can you try games like Catan for free?

**Yes, three of the twelve.** Publishers give away official demos so you can test a game before buying the box:

1. **Carcassonne**: Asmodee's demo with land tiles and rules (free account needed).
2. **Citadels**: Asmodee's character and district cards with demo rules (free account needed).
3. **Pandemic**: Z-Man Games' full Hot Zone – North America print-and-play.

Print tiles and cards in colour on card stock so they shuffle and lie flat, and borrow cubes, pawns and coins from a game you already own. If you own Kingdomino or 7 Wonders Duel, their free add-ons ([The Court](/board-games/kingdomino-the-court/) and the [solo mode](/card-games/7-wonders-duel-solo-print-and-play/)) give the box new life. The rest of our [free printable board games](/board-games/) are listed by players, age and time.

<Callout type="note" title="Before you buy">
Weight and play time say more than the box art. If your group found Catan long, start with Kingdomino, Jaipur or Machi Koro; if they wanted more to chew on, go to Stone Age or 7 Wonders Duel.
</Callout>
