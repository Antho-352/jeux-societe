A paper maze looks like a test of luck, but it isn't. Every maze on this site is built the same way, and that structure gives you a handful of tricks that always find the way out. Here are the five we use, from the one a five-year-old can learn to the one that cracks a 48×48 grid in a few minutes.

## Why every maze here can be solved with a rule

Our mazes are what mathematicians call **perfect mazes**: there is exactly one path between any two points, and there are no loops. You can picture the corridors as the branches of a tree, with the entrance and the exit on two of its twigs. That is also why every wall is attached, directly or through other walls, to the outside edge.

That one property is enough for the first trick to work every time. It also tells you something useful: every wrong turn is a dead end, so wrong turns can be spotted and crossed out.

## Trick 1: the right-hand rule

Put the tip of your pencil, or your finger, against the wall on your right at the entrance. Now move forward without ever losing contact with that wall: into every dead end and back out again, round every corner. You will come out at the exit.

It works because, in a perfect maze, all the walls form one continuous edge. Following it is like walking round the outside of a tree: you visit every branch, and sooner or later you reach the one with the exit. The left hand works just as well; just don't swap hands halfway.

<Callout type="tip" title="Best for young children">
The right-hand rule is the one to teach first. It needs no planning, it always works on our mazes, and children can do it with a finger before they hold a pencil well. Try it on an easy maze with wide paths.
</Callout>

The catch is speed. On a hard maze, following the wall can mean walking almost every corridor in the grid. Tricks 2 to 4 are about skipping most of them.

## Trick 2: start from the exit

Maze designers, and maze generators, tend to put the hardest choices near the entrance. That is where you are tempted by long, promising corridors that go nowhere. The area around the exit is usually less tricky.

Since there is only one path, it does not matter which end you start from. Put your pencil at the exit and work back towards the entrance. When the two ends of your line are close, the last few turns are obvious.

<Callout type="note">
Starting from the end is not cheating. It is the same single path, traced in the other direction. Speed solvers of printed mazes do it as a matter of course.
</Callout>

## Trick 3: fill the dead ends

This is the trick for hard mazes on paper, where you can see the whole grid at once. Scan for any corridor that ends in a closed box. Shade it back to the point where it joins another corridor. Then look again: shading one dead end often turns the corridor it joined into a new dead end.

After two or three passes, the shaded areas cover most of the page and the unshaded path is the solution. It is slower to start than the right-hand rule, but it scales much better as the grid grows.

## Trick 4: look for the long corridor

Hold the page at arm's length. In most hard mazes a few corridors run a long way across the grid without branching much. The solution often uses one of them to cross from one half of the maze to the other.

Find a long corridor that connects to the region near the entrance and to the region near the exit, and you have split one big maze into two smaller ones.

<Callout type="warning" title="Watch out for decoys">
A long corridor that ends in a closed box is the most effective trap a maze can set. Before you commit to one, check both ends.
</Callout>

## Trick 5: mark your choices

When you reach a junction and are not sure, put a small dot on the corridor you take. If it turns out to be a dead end, come back and cross out the dot. You never test the same corridor twice.

This is a paper version of Trémaux's algorithm, a method from the 19th century that works even in mazes with loops. In practice it is what you end up doing naturally on a hard maze; marking it just keeps you honest.

## Which trick to use when

| Situation | Best trick | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A young child's first maze | Right-hand rule | No planning, always works on our mazes |
| Medium maze, want to finish fast | Start from the exit | Skips the traps near the entrance |
| Hard or super hard maze | Fill the dead ends | You see the whole page; shading scales well |
| You are stuck mid-maze | Mark your choices | Never test the same corridor twice |
| Race against a friend | Long corridor + exit start | Splits the maze in two |
