Yahtzee is the dice game most families already own, and the one they wear out first: thirteen turns, the same score card, the same hunt for a large straight. The thirteen games below start with Yahtzee's own ancestors and a free fighting game built on its combinations, which need nothing but five ordinary dice, then move to push-your-luck games, party games and the modern roll-and-write games that grew out of it. Ten of them need nothing but ordinary dice or a free printable, and there is a dice roller on this page if you have no dice at hand.

## Where does Yahtzee come from?

**Yahtzee is a branded version of an older public-domain game called Yacht.** BoardGameGeek's entry for Yahtzee says so directly: "The traditional (public domain) game Yacht predates the trademarked game, and has slightly different scoring." Wikipedia dates Yacht to at least 1938, when it appeared in Clement Wood and Gloria Goddard's *The Complete Book of Games*, and lists Generala and the English game of poker dice among its relatives.

The brand began in 1956. According to Wikipedia, Edwin S. Lowe, the toy maker who had already brought Bingo to the American market, filed Yahtzee as a trademark on April 19, 1956. The story Hasbro tells, as quoted by Wikipedia, is that an anonymous Canadian couple played "The Yacht Game" on their yacht and asked Lowe to make gift sets of it; he bought the rights for 1,000 sets. Milton Bradley bought the E.S. Lowe Company in 1973, and the game now belongs to Hasbro.

**The differences are all on the score card.** BoardGameGeek lists four: Yacht has no Three of a Kind, no bonuses, no Joker rule, and its Full House scores the sum of the dice rather than a flat 25. Yacht's two straights are full five-dice runs worth 30 each, so there is no four-dice small straight either.

## Which dice games are most like Yahtzee?

**Two games keep the exact Yahtzee turn: five dice, up to three rolls, then fill one box.**

**Yacht** is the one to play first. You keep any dice you like between rolls, and after the third roll you must write a score in one empty category, even a zero. The twelve categories are Ones to Sixes, Full House, Four of a Kind, Little Straight (1-2-3-4-5), Big Straight (2-3-4-5-6), Choice and Yacht, which is five of a kind for 50. After twelve turns the highest total wins; the best possible score is 297. It also works solo, as a game against your own record.

<ItemCallout href="/rules/yacht/" note="The twelve categories with examples, how Yacht differs from Yahtzee, and a free printable score sheet for six players per table." />

**Generala** is the Latin American cousin, the most popular version in Ibero-America according to Wikipedia. The categories are shorter: the six numbers, Straight (20), Full House (30), Four of a Kind (40) and Generala, five of a kind, for 50 or 60. A straight, full house or four of a kind on the first roll scores a bonus of 5 or 10. And **if you roll a Generala on your very first roll, you win the game on the spot**, however far behind you are. Write the categories on the back of a Yacht sheet and play tonight.

**Lost Fighters Arena turns the Yahtzee turn into a fight.** This free print-and-play duel by BigBoyShino keeps the five dice and the three rolls, then makes every combination a move: three of a kind, small and large straights, full house, four of a kind and Yahtzee. In contact, three of a kind is a punch; at range, straights are charges that move your fighter across a hexagonal arena before hitting, and the rarer the combination, the harder the hit. The first fighter to drop from 20 HP to zero loses, usually within 15 minutes. Its rulebook even prints the odds of each combination over three rolls: 74% for three of a kind, 5% for a Yahtzee.

## Which games add more risk than Yahtzee?

**In Yahtzee, a bad turn costs you one box. In these games, it can cost you everything you just built.** They are known as push-your-luck games.

**Farkle** keeps Yahtzee's idea of setting dice aside and rolling the rest, but drops the score card. You roll six dice, put aside at least one scoring die (single 1s and 5s, three of a kind, straights), then either bank your running total or roll the remaining dice again. A roll with nothing that scores is a "farkle": the whole turn is lost. Set aside all six dice and you have "hot dice" and may roll all six again. First to 10,000 wins. BoardGameGeek traces the game to the 1930s; it has many names, including Ten Thousand, Zilch and Greed.

<ItemCallout href="/rules/farkle/" note="The full scoring chart, hot dice, the opening threshold and the final round, plus a free printable Farkle score sheet." />

**Pig** is Farkle stripped to one die. Roll as many times as you dare and add each number to your turn total, but a 1 wipes that total out. Hold, and the points are safe. First to 100 wins, and a game takes about ten minutes, which makes [Pig](/rules/pig/) a good way to teach young children the idea of risk before they tackle Farkle.

<ScoreKeeper title="Score keeper for Pig or Farkle" players={['Player 1', 'Player 2', 'Player 3']} />

**Can't Stop** (Sid Sackson, 1980) is the board-game version of the same tension. You roll four dice, split them into two pairs and climb the columns for those totals, from 2 to 12; common totals like 7 have long columns, rare ones like 2 and 12 short ones. Roll again to climb higher, or stop to save your progress. A roll you cannot use ends the turn and erases everything you climbed in it. Claim three columns to win. It plays 2 to 4, ages 9 and up, in about 30 minutes.

**Shut the Box** is the gentlest of the four. Roll two dice and cross out numbers from 1 to 9 that add up to the roll: an 8 could be 8, or 5 and 3, or 1, 3 and 4. When you can no longer make the total, the numbers left standing are your score, and the lowest score wins. Wikipedia notes that the traditional wooden box was a pub game in England, but a pencil does the same job, and every roll is an addition exercise for children.

## Which dice games are best for a crowd?

**Yahtzee seats up to ten on BoardGameGeek, but everyone waits for their turn.** These two keep the whole table involved.

**Bunco** is the classic party dice game for twelve players at three tables of four. In each of six rounds you roll three dice looking for the round number: one point per matching die, and 21 points for a "Bunco", all three showing the round number. There are no decisions at all, which is the point: people talk, swap tables and shout. Our [Bunco rules](/rules/bunco/) explain the head table, partners and how to run a game for fewer players.

**Liar's Dice** turns five dice into a bluffing game. Everyone rolls under a cup and peeks only at their own dice. Players take turns raising a bid about the whole table, such as "seven fives", until someone calls the last bidder a liar; the cups come up and the loser of the challenge loses a die. A commercial edition called Bluff won the Spiel des Jahres in 1993, according to BoardGameGeek, but [Liar's Dice](/rules/liars-dice/) itself is traditional and needs only five dice and a cup per player.

## What are roll-and-write games, Yahtzee's grandchildren?

**A roll-and-write is a game where you roll dice and mark the results on your own sheet**, exactly what you do on a Yahtzee score card. BoardGameGeek files Yahtzee itself in its roll-and-write family. The modern ones go further and keep every player busy on every roll, not just on their own turn.

**Qwixx** (Steffen Benndorf, 2012) was nominated for the Spiel des Jahres in 2013. The active player rolls six dice: two white and four colored. Everyone may cross off the sum of the two white dice in one of their four colored rows, and the active player may also combine a white and a colored die. Numbers must be crossed left to right, so every mark closes off the ones you skipped. It plays 2 to 5, ages 8 and up, in 15 minutes.

**That's Pretty Clever!** (Wolfgang Warsch, 2018; *Ganz schön clever* in German) was nominated for the 2018 Kennerspiel des Jahres. You roll six colored dice three times and keep one die each time, but every die lower than the one you choose goes to the other players, who use it on their own sheets. Each color scores differently and unlocks bonuses in the others, so a good turn sets off a chain. It plays 1 to 4 in about 30 minutes.

### Free roll-and-writes to print

You do not have to buy a pad. Two solo roll-and-writes in our catalogue are free to download from their designers.

**Hall of the Dwarven King** (metalsnail, 2017) gives you seven ordinary dice, each one the mood of a dwarf. You assign them to digging and building, build taverns so they work harder, and score your palace at the end. It fits on one sheet with the rules.

<ItemCallout href="/roll-and-write/hall-of-the-dwarven-king/" note="A one-page solo roll-and-write for seven dice, with a double sheet to save paper. Free on itch.io; pay what you like." />

**Hide nor Hare** (D. Teuber, 2022) is closer to a sudoku. Roll three dice to choose rows and columns, then place friends, trees and pigeons on a 6×6 park so that every pigeon sees the right number of friends. It won Best PnP in the BoardGameGeek 2022 Solitaire Print and Play Contest.
