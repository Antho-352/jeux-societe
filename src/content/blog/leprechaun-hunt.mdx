A leprechaun hunt turns St. Patrick's Day morning into a mystery. Children wake up to tiny green footprints, a chair knocked over and a note from a leprechaun who has hidden his gold, then follow his clues around the house until they find the pot. Below is everything you need for a leprechaun scavenger hunt at home or in class: how to stage the mischief, 12 rhyming clues ready to copy, a classroom plan, and free printables to slot in as extra stations.

## What is a leprechaun hunt?

**A leprechaun hunt is a treasure hunt with a St. Patrick's Day story.** A chain of clues, each pointing to the hiding place of the next, leads to a pot of gold, and the "leprechaun" leaves signs of his visit along the way. Some families do it on the morning of March 17; teachers often run it when the class comes back from recess and finds the room turned upside down.

The story comes from Irish folklore. Wikipedia describes the leprechaun as a small supernatural being, usually a little bearded man in a coat and hat who gets up to mischief, and in later tales a shoemaker with a hidden pot of gold at the end of the rainbow. In the oldest known story, an eighth-century tale, three of these creatures are captured by a king and grant him three wishes to be set free. That makes the theme easy to explain to children: leprechauns are tricky, they love gold, and if you catch one (or his trail), the gold is yours.

<Callout type="note" title="Why green?">
Before the 20th century, leprechauns were usually described in red coats. Green came later, along with the custom of wearing green or a shamrock on St. Patrick's Day; legend says Saint Patrick used the three-leaved shamrock to explain the Holy Trinity.
</Callout>

## How to set up a leprechaun hunt at home

**Do it the night before, in three steps: hide the gold, hide the clues backwards, then add the mischief.**

1. **Hide the pot of gold first.** A flowerpot, a small bucket or a mug painted black works. Put it somewhere off the main path, such as under a bed or in the laundry room.
2. **Hide the clues in reverse order.** Put the last clue (the one that points to the gold) somewhere else, then the clue that points to that spot, and so on. You end up holding clue 1, which the leprechaun "leaves" on the breakfast table.
3. **Write the list down.** Number each hiding place on your phone as you go, so you can rescue a stuck hunter.
4. **Add the mischief last.** This is the part children remember: a trail of small footprints, a tipped-over chair, a few books on the floor, a tiny note in the fridge.
5. **Keep the trail safe.** Nothing near the stove, up a ladder, or behind something heavy that could fall.

For footprints, cut a small foot shape from green card stock and trace it a dozen times, or press the side of your fist in washable green paint on paper and add toes with a fingertip. Paper footprints taped down are easier to clear than paint on the floor.

## Clues that lead to the pot of gold

**A good clue describes one hiding place in two short lines, and the rhyme lands on that place.** Written in the leprechaun's voice, it keeps the story going. Use places every home has, read each clue aloud before you hide it, and swap any spot your house does not have.

1. I sneaked a snack and left the door ajar: look where the milk and the juice cartons are. *(fridge)*
2. I washed my tiny green boots with care: look in the bathtub, the next clue is there. *(bathtub)*
3. I bounced on the cushions, I'm sorry to say: look under the couch where I rested today. *(couch)*
4. I tried on your sneakers, they're far too big: look by the shoes where I danced a jig. *(shoe rack)*
5. I read all your books to learn how to spell: the bookshelf is hiding a clue as well. *(bookshelf)*
6. I left some gold dust where you sleep: under your pillow, take a peep. *(pillow)*
7. I hid in the laundry so you couldn't see: look in the basket of clothes for me. *(laundry basket)*
8. I borrowed your toothbrush to polish my gold: check by the sink, if the truth must be told. *(bathroom sink)*
9. I played with your blocks and your teddy bear too: lift the toy box lid for one more clue. *(toy box)*
10. I spun in the washer, round and round: open its door, a clue will be found. *(washing machine)*
11. I sat at your table and ate all the crumbs: look under the table, here the next clue comes. *(kitchen table)*
12. You followed my trail, you're quick and bold: under your bed is my pot of gold! *(final clue: under the bed)*

Clue 12 always goes last; the others can be in any order. Short of time? Spin the picker and copy one clue at a time.

## A leprechaun hunt for the classroom

**St. Patrick's Day 2027 is a Wednesday, so many children will spend it at school.** A classroom leprechaun scavenger hunt fits into a single class period and works for kindergarten to about third grade.

- **Stage it while the class is out**, at recess or lunch: a few chairs pushed back, footprints from the window, a note on the board in tiny handwriting.
- **Hide 6 to 10 clues at classroom landmarks**: the class library, the pencil sharpener, the coat hooks or cubbies, the globe, the teacher's desk, the sink, the classroom door. The last three clues in the picker above are written for a classroom.
- **Split the class into teams of four or five** and let each team read one clue aloud in turn, so everyone gets a go and nobody races ahead.
- **Make the gold shared**: one pot for the whole class, opened together. It avoids arguments about who found what.
- **Check your school's food policy** before putting treats in the pot; stickers, pencils or a class read-aloud book avoid the question.

A day-before activity extends the fun: let small groups build a leprechaun trap from a shoe box, paper and tape, then find it "sprung" and empty the next morning, with clue 1 inside. ## What to put in the pot of gold

**Anything gold, green or rainbow-colored works, and it does not need to be candy.** Ideas that children enjoy:

- **Stickers, temporary tattoos or a shamrock pencil** for a classroom pot.
- **Rocks painted gold** the day before (large enough not to fit in a small child's mouth).
- **Rainbow crayons or markers**, or a book about Ireland or rainbows.
- **Chocolate coins in gold foil** for school-age children.
- **A note from the leprechaun** promising to come back next year.

<Callout type="warning" title="Toddlers and small treasures">
The American Academy of Pediatrics lists coins, marbles, small balls and toys with small parts among household choking hazards, and advises keeping hard, gooey or sticky candy from children until age 4 or older. If a toddler is joining the hunt, fill their pot with stickers, board books or large toys instead.
</Callout>
