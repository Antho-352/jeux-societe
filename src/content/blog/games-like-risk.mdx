People who search for games like Risk usually love the map and the armies but not the four-hour evening that ends with one player sulking on the sofa since round two. Below are twelve war and territory games, sorted by the Risk problem each one fixes, with players, time and BoardGameGeek weight for every pick. Five of them are free to print tonight, and at the end you will find Risk's real battle odds and a quiz that picks your next war game.

## What does Risk do well, and what goes wrong?

**Risk gets the fantasy right: a world map, your armies, and a plan to take a continent.** Albert Lamorisse's game, sold by Parker Brothers from 1959, is easy to teach. Each turn you collect reinforcements, attack neighbouring territories with dice, then make one free move. Holding a whole continent earns bonus armies, and trading sets of Risk cards earns more each time.

The complaints are just as well known, and they all come from the rules:

- **It is long.** In World Domination Risk you win only by holding all 42 territories.
- **Players are knocked out.** A player who loses their last army is out, and the others keep playing.
- **Dice decide battles.** The attacker rolls up to three dice, the defender up to two, and ties go to the defender.

## Which games are like Risk but shorter?

**Small World keeps the conquest and drops the elimination.** Philippe Keyaerts's game (Days of Wonder, 2009) gives you a random fantasy race paired with a special power, from 14 races and 20 powers. You conquer regions, score one coin for each region you hold at the end of your turn, and when your empire runs out of steam you put it **into decline** and pick a new race. After the final round, the most coins wins, so nobody sits out. It plays two to five in 40 to 80 minutes, from age 8.

<ItemCallout href="/board-games/small-world-lost-tribes-crusade/" note="Asmodee's free mini expansion for solo and two-player Small World: you face the Lost Tribes, an opponent run by simple rules. Needs the base game." />

**Kemet is Risk that rewards attacking, with no dice.** In Jacques Bariot and Guillaume Montiage's game (Matagot, 2012), each player leads an Egyptian tribe. You score by winning attacks, holding temples and building pyramids, and the game ends at 8 or 10 victory points. Battles are settled with cards from a six-card hand that shrinks as you use it, so you can plan which fights to win. It is heavier than Risk (3.01) but listed at 90 to 120 minutes.

## Which strategy games like Risk have less luck?

**Diplomacy has no luck at all.** Allan B. Calhamer's game, also from 1959, puts seven players in charge of the great powers of Europe before World War I. Each season everyone writes secret orders for their armies and fleets, then all orders are revealed together. The larger supported force wins. There are no dice, so the game is decided by the deals you make, and break. BoardGameGeek lists six hours and rates seven players best: it is less luck, not less time.

**Inis** (Christian Martinez, Matagot, 2016) moves the fight to a Celtic island. Each round players draft a hand of action cards, then use them to add clans, move and build. You can win by **leadership** (leading territories that hold six rival clans), **land** (being present in six territories) or **religion** (territories holding six sanctuaries), so a player who looks harmless can win suddenly. Two to four players, 60 to 90 minutes.

**Root** (Cole Wehrle, Leder Games, 2018) is a woodland war in which each side has its own abilities and its own way to win. The Marquise de Cat has seized the forest to harvest its riches, the Eyrie birds want their old domain back, the Woodland Alliance works to undermine the cats, and the Vagabond wanders the paths between them. It won BoardGameGeek's Golden Geek Board Game of the Year for 2018, and at 3.84 it is the heaviest game on this list.

## What are the best two-player war games like Risk?

Risk's rulebook has a two-player variant with a neutral army, but these games are designed for two from the start.

**Memoir '44** (Richard Borg, Days of Wonder, 2004) replays World War II battles such as Omaha Beach and Pegasus Bridge on a hex board. You order units by playing command cards, then roll custom dice to attack. Scenarios take 30 to 45 minutes, so you can play both sides in one evening. BoardGameGeek voters rate two as its best count.

**Twilight Struggle** (Ananda Gupta and Jason Matthews, GMT Games, 2005) is the Cold War from 1945 to 1989 for exactly two. The map is the world of the period, and most of the fight is over influence, country by country, played through the cards in your hand. If nobody has won outright by the end of turn 10, points decide. It won the Golden Geek for best two-player game and best wargame in 2006. Allow two to three hours.

Two free duels are in our catalogue. **Narrow Seas** by Felix Livni is a 30-minute Age of Sail fight: you secretly assign crew on two ships, then sail and fire with cards whose suit must match the hex your ship is on. It placed third overall in BoardGameGeek's 2025 two-player print-and-play contest. **Melee**, below, is a gladiator fight on a hex arena. More are on our [two-player games](/board-games/2-player/) page, and if you also play chess, our [chess vs checkers](/blog/chess-vs-checkers/) guide compares the two classic war games on a grid.

## Which free printable war games can you play tonight?

**Five war games in our catalogue cost nothing.** Siege of Paris needs only a printer, scissors and tape; the others ask for more cutting or a free account.

**Bloc by Bloc: Uprising** (Greg Loring-Albright and T.L. Simons, Outlandish Games) is a city uprising for one to four players. You lead workers, students, neighbours or prisoners, build barricades, occupy districts and try to liberate the city before the military arrives. It is cooperative and semi-cooperative: the table fights the police together while each faction keeps its own ambitions. Its Creative Commons licence lets us host the files.

<ItemCallout href="/board-games/bloc-by-bloc-uprising/" note="The official 24-page rulebook and 15 pages of A4 district tiles. 1 to 4 players aged 14 and up, 60 to 180 minutes." />

**Siege of Paris** is a hunt-style war game themed on the siege of 1870 to 1871. A small force holds the central citadel against two invading armies; turns go defender, silver, defender, black. The invaders win with one officer and three soldiers inside the citadel, the defender by capturing 24 soldiers and 6 officers. It prints on four Letter pages.

<ItemCallout href="/board-games/printable-siege-of-paris/" note="Board in two parts, cut-out pieces for three sides and the rules. Two to five players from age 12; add tape and scissors." />

**The Fantasy Trip: Melee** is Steve Jackson's arena-combat game, now a free PDF from Steve Jackson Games. You build a fighter by splitting points between strength and dexterity, buy weapons and armour, and fight on a hex map. To hit, roll three dice: equal to or under your adjusted dexterity is a hit. It is Risk's battle zoomed in to one fighter per side.

<ItemCallout href="/board-games/the-fantasy-trip-melee/" note="The $0 PDF from Warehouse 23 (free account needed). Two players from age 10, 30 to 60 minutes, three six-sided dice." />

**Ultima Ratio Regis** is the answer for players who want Risk to last for weeks. Ignacio Torres Siles's grand strategy game covers Europe from 1480 to 1790 for one to eight players, in scenarios or a full campaign of 20 turns. The publisher estimates two to four hours per turn, and building it means a 97 x 69 cm map and about 800 counters. [See the files and build notes](/board-games/ultima-ratio-regis/).

## What are the odds in a Risk battle?

Risk's dice look simple, but the defender's tie rule shapes the whole game. **We computed every combination** of the dice under the 1993 rules: compare the highest die of each side, then the second highest; the higher die wins and ties go to the defender.

| Attacker vs defender dice | Attacker wins all | One each | Defender wins all |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 vs 2 | 37.2% | 33.6% | 29.3% |
| 3 vs 1 | 66.0% | n/a | 34.0% |
| 2 vs 2 | 22.8% | 32.4% | 44.8% |
| 2 vs 1 | 57.9% | n/a | 42.1% |
| 1 vs 2 | 25.5% | n/a | 74.5% |
| 1 vs 1 | 41.7% | n/a | 58.3% |

Three takeaways. **Always attack with three dice when you can**: against two defence dice, the attacker loses 0.92 armies per roll on average and the defender 1.08. **Two against two favours the defender**, who wins both armies 44.8% of the time. And a lone attacker against one defender loses more often than not.
