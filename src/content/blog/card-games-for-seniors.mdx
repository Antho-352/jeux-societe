Card games for seniors have one big advantage over most activities: a deck fits in a pocket and goes anywhere, from the kitchen table to a care home lounge or a hospital visit. The best ones need no fast reflexes, keep the hand small and the rules short, and leave room to talk. Below are 10 card games for older adults, from the easiest to Canasta, with why each suits older players, how to adapt it, and our full rules with free score sheets.

## What makes a card game senior-friendly?

**There is no "seniors' card game": a lifelong bridge player and someone who has not held a card in 40 years need different things.** Five questions sort most games quickly.

- **Can everyone see the cards?** Small corner indexes are the most common problem. Large-print decks, good light and a contrasting tablecloth help.
- **Can everyone hold the cards?** Fanning 13 cards is hard for stiff or shaky hands. Games with 3 to 7 cards, or a card holder, solve it.
- **How much must you remember?** Some games ask you to track every card played; others only the top card of the pile.
- **Is it a race?** Games won by slapping or grabbing (Slapjack, Spoons, Speed) are fun with grandchildren but frustrating for slower hands.
- **Does it suit the group?** Two players, a table of four or a whole lounge all call for different games.

### 1. Crazy Eights: the easiest start

**Match the top card of the pile by suit or by rank; eights are wild.** Everyone holds 5 cards (7 with two players), the only decision is which card to play, and a round takes a few minutes. It is the game to start with for someone who has not played in years, and it works across generations. See the [Crazy Eights rules](/rules/crazy-eights/).

### 2. Thirty-One: three cards and a knock

**Collect cards of one suit worth as close to 31 as possible.** You only ever hold three cards, so it is the easiest game here to hold and to see. Each player starts with three lives (counters or coins), which keeps everyone in the game for a while, and it seats up to nine. It is also known as Scat. Read the [Thirty-One rules](/rules/thirty-one/).

### 3. Golf: cards on the table, not in hand

**Six cards lie face down in two rows of three, and you swap them for lower ones.** Nothing is held in the hand, which makes Golf a good choice for stiff fingers, and the lowest total after nine "holes" wins. Avoid the four-card version if memory is a concern: there you may look at your cards only once. See the [Golf rules](/rules/golf/).

### 4. Rummy: the classic everyone half remembers

**Draw, discard, and lay down runs (4-5-6 of hearts) and sets (three 7s).** Rummy is one of the best-known card games, so many players have met some version of it and the rules come back quickly. Deal 10 cards with two players, 7 with three or four, and 6 with five or six; the smaller hands of a bigger group are easier to hold. Our [Rummy rules](/rules/rummy/) come with a free score sheet.

### 5. Gin Rummy: the two-player favorite

**Rummy for two, where you knock when your unmatched cards total 10 or less.** It is quick, quiet and endlessly replayable, a natural game for a couple or a weekly visit. Read the [Gin Rummy rules](/rules/gin-rummy/).

### 6. Cribbage: counting fifteens on a pegboard

**Play cards toward a count of 31, then score your hand for fifteens, pairs and runs; first to 121 wins.** According to Wikipedia, John Aubrey credited the English poet Sir John Suckling with inventing it in the early 17th century. The pegboard keeps the score, so nobody has to add up totals at the end, and the counting keeps the mind busy. Our [Cribbage rules](/rules/cribbage/) include a score sheet, and the [printable cribbage board](/board-games/printable-cribbage-board/) replaces a wooden one.

<ItemCallout href="/rules/cribbage/" note="Two-player Cribbage step by step: the crib, the starter, pegging to 31 and scoring the hand, with a free score sheet." />

### 7. Hearts: avoid the queen

**Try not to win tricks with hearts in them, and above all the queen of spades.** In a trick-taking game, each player plays one card and the highest card of the suit led wins them all. Hearts needs no partner; the standard game is for four, each holding 13 cards. Read the [Hearts rules](/rules/hearts/).

### 8. Euchre: five cards, four friends

**A partnership game with a short 24-card deck and only five tricks a hand.** The small hand makes Euchre easier to hold than most trick-taking games, and the first team to 10 points wins. Wikipedia notes it was once regarded as the national card game of the United States and keeps a strong following in the Midwest. See the [Euchre rules](/rules/euchre/).

### 9. Spades: bid and make it

**Spades are always trumps, and each player bids how many tricks they will take.** It is played in partnerships, so a stronger player can pair up with someone less sure. Thirteen cards make a large hand: use a card holder. Read the [Spades rules](/rules/spades/).

### 10. Canasta: the reward for keen players

**A partnership rummy with two decks and four jokers (108 cards), where seven-card melds called canastas score big.** Wikipedia says it was devised in Montevideo, Uruguay, in 1939 and became rapidly popular in the United States in the 1950s, when card sets, card trays and books were produced for it. Games are long and the rules are the most detailed on this list, so it suits a regular group. Our [Canasta rules](/rules/canasta/) explain melds, the frozen pile and scoring, with a score sheet.

## Card games for players with memory loss

**Cards can stay part of life after a diagnosis of dementia, if the game is adapted.** The Alzheimer's Association gives carers advice that applies directly to cards: focus on enjoyment, not achievement; break the activity into simple, easy-to-follow steps; and if attention wanes or frustration grows, it is time to end or change the activity.

- **Choose one simple action per turn.** [War](/rules/war/) needs no decisions at all: both players turn a card and the higher one wins. Crazy Eights asks only "same suit or same number?".
- **Play with open hands.** Lay the cards face up on the table so a partner can help, and drop the scoring if it causes stress.
- **Use what the player remembers.** A lifelong Rummy or Cribbage player may still enjoy a simplified game with a partner helping, even when new rules no longer stick.
- **Keep it short**, and stop while it is still fun.

Skip-Bo, Mattel's sequencing game, is another option for players who find numbers easier than suits: you build piles from 1 to 12 in order. Wikipedia notes it can also be played with three regular decks, aces to queens counting 1 to 12 and the kings and jokers as wild cards. See our [Skip-Bo rules](/rules/skip-bo/).

## Two-player games for couples and visits

**For a pair, the classics are Gin Rummy and Cribbage**: both are quick, both have a free score sheet, and both reward years of practice. Two players can also play Rummy with 10 cards each, or a two-handed Crazy Eights with 7.

**For someone who plays alone**, solitaire is a good daily habit. [Klondike](/rules/klondike-solitaire/) is the one most people know; our guide to the [types of solitaire](/blog/types-of-solitaire/) has easier and harder ones. And for a group of three, see our [card games for 3 people](/blog/card-games-for-3-people/).

## Large print cards, card holders and other adaptations

**Most problems at the card table are about seeing and holding, and both have simple fixes.** VisionAware, the low-vision service of the American Printing House for the Blind, suggests:

- **A card holder**, which acts as an "extra hand" and frees the player to use a magnifier or spread the cards out. A shoebox lid with a slit cut along it, or a block of wood with a groove, does the same job.
- **Contrast**: a white card shows up much better on a dark placemat or tablecloth.
- **Good light**: an adjustable flex-arm or gooseneck lamp aimed at the table, with no glare.
- **Large-print or braille decks**, which many companies make, or cards labeled by hand with a bold marker.
- **A magnifier**: a low-vision specialist can say whether a chest, neck or gooseneck magnifier would help.

**You can also print your own larger deck.** The free [printable playing card deck](/card-games/printable-playing-card-deck/) we list has 52 cards and two jokers, four to a US Letter page. Printed at 129% on 11 × 17 in tabloid paper (11 ÷ 8.5 = 1.29), each card is about 29% taller and wider, which is about two-thirds more card. On A3 paper from A4, use 141%. Print on card stock and round the corners so they last.

Other small changes help too: shuffle and deal for a player with arthritis, use a discard tray so cards do not slide, and keep the score sheet in large handwriting on the table where everyone can see it.
