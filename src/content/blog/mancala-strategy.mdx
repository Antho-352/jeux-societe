The best mancala strategy comes down to one habit: **count the seeds in a pit and compare them with its distance to your store.** That count tells you which move gives a free turn, which one captures, and which one hands your opponent a capture. The seven tips below teach it on real board positions, from the best opening to the endgame. They use the Kalah rules found in most Western mancala sets, and every claim was checked with a small program that plays those rules.

## 1. Count to your store: the free-turn rule

**If the last seed lands in your own store, you play again.** Whether that happens is simple arithmetic: pit 1 is 6 steps from your store, pit 2 is 5 steps, down to pit 6, which is 1 step away. A pit gives a free turn when its number of seeds equals its number of steps.

<Figure src="/img/blog/mancala-strategy/count-to-store.svg" alt="Mancala starting position with four seeds in every pit; pit 3 is highlighted and the steps to the store are 6, 5, 4, 3, 2 and 1 from pit 1 to pit 6" caption="At the start, only pit 3 has as many seeds (4) as steps to the store (4)." width={1060} height={330} />

With a big pile, add 13: the seeds go all the way round (your 6 pits, your store, the opponent's 6 pits) and come back, so 19 seeds in pit 1 also end in your store. You will rarely need this, but it is why a huge pile is not useless.

How much is the habit worth? We had a computer play two styles against each other, 100,000 games per pairing, seats alternating. **A player that always takes a free turn when one exists, and otherwise moves at random, beat a fully random player in 89% of games.** One rule, nothing else.

## 2. Opening moves that give a free turn

**Open with pit 3, then play pit 6.** Pit 3 holds four seeds and is four steps from your store, so it earns a free turn. Pit 6 then puts one more seed in your store and spreads the rest on your opponent's side.

<Figure src="/img/blog/mancala-strategy/opening-3-6.svg" alt="Two boards: after pit 3 your store holds 1 seed and you move again; after pit 6 your store holds 2 and the opponent's first four pits hold 5 seeds each" caption="The opening 3 then 6. Your store holds 2 seeds and pit 3 is empty, ready for a capture later." width={1060} height={300} />

This is not just a habit. Kalah has been **solved**: in 2000, Geoffrey Irving, Jeroen Donkers and Jos Uiterwijk proved with a computer that the first player wins Kalah with four seeds per pit by 10 seeds with perfect play (under the rule they used, where ending in any empty pit of yours captures). The perfect game they publish opens with pit 3, then pit 6. Wikipedia also reports a later analysis by Mark Rawlings for the standard rule: there, 3-then-6 wins by 8 and is the best of the ten possible first turns, while opening with pit 1 loses by 14.

Pit 3 then pit 6 also leaves pit 3 empty, a capture threat for later in the game.

## 3. Chain extra turns, nearest the store first

**When several pits give a free turn, play the one nearest your store first.** Sowing from a pit adds a seed to every pit after it, so playing a far pit first spoils the count of the nearer ones. Playing the near ones first leaves the far ones untouched.

<Figure src="/img/blog/mancala-strategy/chain-extra-turns.svg" alt="Board with 4, 0, 0, 3, 2 and 1 seeds in your pits; the order 6, 5, 6, 4, 6, 5 chains five free turns and ends with a capture" caption="Pits 4, 5 and 6 hold 3, 2 and 1 seeds. Playing 6, 5, 6, 4, 6 gives five free turns in a row; our program confirms it is the best turn here." width={1060} height={320} />

In the diagram, starting with pit 4 would push seeds into pits 5 and 6 (3 and 2 seeds), and the chain stops after one free turn. Wikipedia notes that the longest possible chain on a six-pit row is 17 moves; you will not see that in a real game, but chains of three or four are common.

## 4. Captures: when to empty a pit, when to defend

**If your last seed lands in an empty pit on your side, and the pit opposite holds seeds, you take them all, plus your own seed.** Captures are where big swings come from.

<Figure src="/img/blog/mancala-strategy/capture.svg" alt="Before and after: the single seed in pit 2 lands in empty pit 3, which takes the 7 seeds opposite plus itself into your store" caption="Pit 2 holds one seed, pit 3 is empty and 7 seeds sit opposite: play pit 2 and capture 8." width={1060} height={300} />

To attack, keep an empty pit on your side with a pit behind it that can reach it, and wait for your opponent to build a pile opposite. To defend, look at **their** empty pits: the pit of yours opposite an empty pit is in danger whenever one of their pits can end its sowing there. You can move the threatened pile away, or drop a seed into their empty pit so it is no longer empty.

This second habit is worth as much as the first. In our tests, a player that picks the turn banking the most seeds ("greedy") beat the free-turn-only player 82% of the time. A "careful" player that also subtracts the most the opponent can take back on the next turn beat the greedy one **80% of the time**.

## 5. Late in the game: count who runs out first

The game ends as soon as all six pits on one side are empty, and **the other player puts every seed left on their side in their own store**. That rule decides many close games.

- Before you play your last seeds, count the seeds still on your opponent's side: they are theirs.
- If your opponent is about to run out, the seeds on your side will be yours. Keep them there rather than sowing them across.
- Running out yourself is only good when you are already ahead by more than what remains on their side.

The common advice to "hoard" seeds needs that count. We tested a careful player that, once 20 or fewer seeds were left on the board, also preferred keeping seeds on its own side. Against the same careful player without that habit, it **won 45% of games and lost 49%**. Hoarding pays only when the count says the other side will run out first.

## 6. Playing second: how to catch up

The first player has the edge, and it grows as both players get better. In our simulations, the first player won 48% of games between two random players (45% for the second, the rest drawn), 59% between two careful players, and 72% between two greedy ones. Kalah with four seeds is, after all, a proven first-player win.

When you move second:

- **Answer threats before chasing your own plans.** Check which of your pits sits opposite one of their empty pits.
- **Do not hand back free turns.** Every seed you sow on their side can change which of their pits now counts exactly to their store.
- **Keep your empty pits.** An empty pit with seeds opposite is your capture chance once they make a mistake.
- **Make it fair.** Alternate who starts, or use the pie rule from our rules page: after the first move, the second player may swap sides.

## Practice on a printed board

Strategy sticks when you move the seeds yourself. Set up the diagrams on this page with beans or buttons, play them from both sides, then play a few games trying one tip at a time.

<Callout type="example" title="How we measured">
We wrote a Kalah program with the rules on our [mancala rules page](/rules/mancala/) (four seeds per pit; a capture needs seeds in the opposite pit; leftover seeds go to the owner of that side) and played 100,000 games for each pairing, with players swapping seats every other game. "Random" picks any legal pit. "Free turn" takes a free turn when it can (nearest the store first), otherwise plays at random. "Greedy" tries every possible full turn, chains included, and keeps the one that banks the most seeds. "Careful" does the same but subtracts the most the opponent can bank on the next turn. The quiz answers and the diagrams were checked with the same program.
</Callout>
