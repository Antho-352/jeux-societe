Five is the age when board games start to work: a child can count the squares, wait for a turn and finish a whole game. The trick is to choose games that fit what a five-year-old can actually do, not the age on the box alone. Below are twelve first games, sorted by type, with players, age and time for each, plus the free boards, card games and puzzles in our catalogue that you can print tonight.

## What can a 5-year-old handle in a board game?

**More than you might think, for about ten minutes at a time.** The CDC's milestones list what most children (75% or more) do by a given age. By five, that includes following rules or taking turns when playing games with other children, counting to 10, naming some numbers between 1 and 5 when you point to them, and paying attention for 5 to 10 minutes during an activity.

The American Academy of Pediatrics' parent site, HealthyChildren.org, adds that four- and five-year-olds can count 10 or more objects, name at least four colors and are "more likely to agree to rules". Together, that gives a simple brief for a first game:

- **numbers up to 6** on a die or spinner, or colors instead of numbers;
- **no reading**: the CDC milestone at five is naming some letters, not reading words;
- **short rounds**, 10 to 20 minutes at most;
- **a clear turn order** and one action per turn.

**Board games are good practice, not just fun.** The CDC's own tips for five-year-olds suggest teaching your child to follow rules with simple board games, card games or Simon Says. In a 2008 study, preschoolers from low-income families, with a mean age of 5.4, who played a numbered board game for roughly an hour improved at counting, recognising numbers, comparing quantities and placing numbers on a line, and the gains were still there nine weeks later. Children who played the same game with colored squares instead of numbers did not improve.

## Which cooperative games are best for a first game?

**Start cooperative, so nobody loses alone.** In these games the players form one team against the board. A parent can think out loud on every turn, and the child learns the rules without the sting of losing to a grown-up.

**Hoot Owl Hoot!** (Susan McKinley Ross, Peaceable Kingdom, 2010) has everyone hold color cards face up. Play a card to fly an owl to the next space of that color; draw a sun card and the sun moves closer to dawn. Get every owl home before sunrise and you all win. It needs color matching, not counting.

**Race to the Treasure!** (Gina Manola, Peaceable Kingdom, 2012) is a path-building game. Each turn you draw a tile from the bag: a piece of path, which you place together toward three keys and the treasure, or an ogre, who moves one step closer. It needs no reading.

**Outfoxed!** (Gamewright, 2014) is a first whodunit: roll to move and find clues, then check suspects with an evidence scanner before the guilty fox reaches the exit. It is listed from age 5 and is the most "grown-up" game in this group.

**Count Your Chickens!** (Peggy Brown, Peaceable Kingdom, 2010) is the counting game. Spin, move Mother Hen, and collect one baby chick for each space she travelled, counting out loud together. Land on the fox and one chick leaves the coop. **Orchard** (HABA, 1986) is the classic: pick fruit from the trees as a team before the raven arrives. Both are listed from age 3, so they suit a five-year-old with a younger sibling.

For more family co-ops, from age 4 to grown-up campaigns, see our guide to [cooperative board games](/blog/cooperative-board-games/).

## Which classic board games can you print free?

**Race games on a numbered track are the classic first board game**, and they use the same kind of numbered track as the counting studies. Candy Land (Eleanor Abbott, Milton Bradley, 1949) uses color cards instead of numbers, which is fine at four. At five, a numbered board does more for counting.

**Snakes and Ladders** is the obvious one: roll one die, count the squares out loud, climb ladders and slide down snakes to square 100. There is no decision to make, so a five-year-old can beat a parent fair and square.

## Which matching and stacking games suit five-year-olds?

**Zingo!** (2002) is bingo with pictures. The holder dispenses two picture tiles; whoever has that picture on their card calls it and takes the tile, and the first to fill all nine spaces wins. Each card has an easier green side and a harder red side. Turns are simultaneous, so nobody waits.

**Make your own picture bingo for free.** Print our [blank 3×3 bingo card](/bingo/blank-3x3-bingo-card-template/), draw the same nine pictures on every card in a different order, and call them from a hat. Nine squares means a game lasts a couple of minutes.

**Animal Upon Animal** (Klaus Miltenberger, HABA, 2005) has you stack 29 wooden animals into a wobbly pyramid; the first player to place all their animals wins. **Rhino Hero** (HABA, 2011) builds a card tower: you lay walls and a roof card each turn, and whoever makes the tower collapse loses. Both reward steady hands more than age, so a five-year-old can win honestly.

**Dragomino** (Bruno Cathala, Marie Fort and Wilfried Fort, Blue Orange, 2020) is Kingdomino for younger players. You pick domino-shaped land tiles and match landscapes; each match lets you draw an egg, which may hold a baby dragon worth a point. It won the Kinderspiel des Jahres, Germany's children's game of the year award, in 2021.

## How do you play board games with a 5-year-old without tears?

Losing is part of every competitive game, and these habits keep it small:

1. **Open with a cooperative game.** If the team loses, nobody lost alone, and you can try again at once.
2. **Keep games short.** Pick games that end inside the 5 to 10 minutes of attention the CDC milestones describe, and play twice rather than once long.
3. **Count out loud together.** It slows the game down, shows the rule, and is the counting practice the studies measured.
4. **Lose out loud yourself.** "Oh no, a snake! Never mind, next game." Children copy how the adults react.
5. **Do not let them win every time.** Luck-only games like Snakes and Ladders or War already give a five-year-old a fair chance against you.
6. **Stop while it is still fun.** End before the child is tired, not after the meltdown.

The AAP's clinical report on play notes that playing with parents and peers builds social-emotional, cognitive, language and self-regulation skills. Those skills are built game by game, not in one evening. For a whole evening built around games, see our [family game night ideas](/blog/family-game-night-ideas/).
