The games most like chess are its own relatives. Xiangqi in China, shogi in Japan, janggi in Korea and makruk in Thailand share chess's idea of two armies and a king to trap, and chess historians trace them all to the same Indian ancestor. Below are those four, then three simpler games that play like chess (the Jungle Game, checkers and Fox and Geese) and three modern two-player games chess players enjoy. For each: the board, the pieces, what changes from chess, and how hard it is to pick up. Five of the ten have a free board on our site, so you can try one tonight with paper pieces.

## The chess family: one ancestor, many cousins

**Chaturanga, first known in India around the 7th century, is the common ancestor of chess, xiangqi, janggi, shogi and makruk, according to the prevailing view among chess historians** summarized by Wikipedia. It became shatranj in Persia, which is the form that reached medieval Europe and turned into modern chess. The Asian branches kept the idea (a general or king, chariots, horses, elephants and foot soldiers) and changed the details.

## The four chess cousins

### 1. Xiangqi (Chinese chess)

**Xiangqi is the most popular board game in China**, according to Wikipedia, and is also widely played in Vietnam. Pieces stand on the intersections of a 9×10 grid rather than in squares. A "river" across the middle stops the elephants, and each general must stay inside its palace, a 3×3 box marked with diagonals, guarded by two advisors.

The piece chess players notice first is the **cannon**, which moves like a rook but must jump over exactly one piece to capture. Another rule surprises everyone: the two generals may never face each other on an open file. The goal, as in chess, is to checkmate the enemy general. BoardGameGeek's community rates xiangqi at 3.55 out of 5 for complexity, close to chess at 3.64.

### 2. Shogi (Japanese chess)

**Shogi's twist is the drop: a piece you capture becomes yours, and instead of moving you may place it back on any empty square.** Wikipedia calls shogi the earliest chess-like game with this rule. Captured material stays in play, so the fight never thins out.

The board is 9×9 with no colors, and each side has 20 wedge-shaped pieces that point towards the opponent, which is how you tell whose they are. Most pieces promote when they reach the far three ranks; the printed piece is simply turned over. BoardGameGeek rates shogi 3.80 for complexity, the highest of the ten here, and lists games of about an hour.

<ItemCallout href="/board-games/printable-shogi/" note="Board, cut-out pieces with their promoted sides in red, a piece guide and a page of rules, on three Letter pages." />

### 3. Janggi (Korean chess)

**Janggi grew out of xiangqi and uses the same 9×10 grid, but without the river.** Wikipedia notes that the pieces are octagonal discs of different sizes, the sides are blue (or green) and red, and the blue side moves first. Wikipedia adds that jumping cannons and long-range elephants can make games fast-paced. If you know xiangqi, most pieces will look familiar; we do not have a janggi printable yet.

### 4. Makruk (Thai chess)

**Makruk is the closest to the chess you know**: an 8×8 board, a king, rooks, knights and pawns. Wikipedia notes two big differences: the "seed", which stands in for the queen, moves only one square diagonally, and pawns (cowrie shells) start on the third rank and promote when they reach the sixth. Cambodia plays essentially the same game under the name ouk.

## Simpler cousins: the Jungle Game and checkers

### 5. The Jungle Game (Dou Shou Qi)

**A first strategy game for children that still interests adults.** The board has 7 columns and 9 rows of squares, with two rivers in the middle and a den guarded by traps at each end. Each player has eight animals ranked from rat to elephant; an animal captures animals of equal or lower rank, except that the rat can take the elephant. Wikipedia describes the win: move any piece into the opponent's den, or capture all their pieces.

For a chess player, it is chess with the piece values made visible: every capture is a comparison of ranks. Lions and tigers leap across the rivers, and only the rat swims.

### 6. Checkers

**Checkers uses the chess board but only one kind of move**: diagonally forward, jumping to capture, with jumps compulsory. It is the gentlest step from chess to a pure tactics game, and BoardGameGeek rates it 1.70 for complexity against 3.64 for chess. Our [chess vs checkers guide](/blog/chess-vs-checkers/) compares the two in detail, and our [checkers strategy guide](/blog/how-to-win-at-checkers/) shows the traps on diagrams.

## Hunt games: Fox and Geese

### 7. Fox and Geese

**Two unequal armies: one fox that can capture, and thirteen geese that cannot but can trap it.** It is played on the 33-point cross-shaped board also used for peg solitaire. The fox jumps over geese to capture, as in checkers; the geese win by hemming the fox in. Wikipedia traces the family of fox games back to Halatafl, mentioned in a 14th-century Icelandic saga, and notes that Fox and Geese was a favorite pastime of Queen Victoria.

Playing both sides teaches what chess players call piece coordination: the geese only succeed as a group.

## Modern games for chess players

These three are recent designs that keep chess's key features: two players at heart, no dice, and every piece in plain sight.

### 8. Hive

**Hive (John Yianni, 2001) has no board: the pieces form the board as you place them.** Each side has eleven pieces shaped as insects, each with its own way of moving, and you win by completely surrounding the opponent's queen bee. Pieces are never captured. BoardGameGeek lists it for 2 players, ages 9 and up, about 20 minutes, with a complexity of 2.31; it won a Mensa Select award in 2006. The pieces are hexagonal tiles and need only a flat surface, which makes it a good travel game.

### 9. Onitama

**Onitama (Shimpei Sato, 2014) is chess on a 5×5 board, cut down to the essentials.** Each player has a master and four students. The moves come from five cards: you hold two, each showing a movement pattern, and the card you use goes to your opponent's side for their next turn while you take the spare one. You win by capturing the opponent's master or moving your master onto their master's starting square, the Temple Arch. BoardGameGeek gives 15 to 20 minutes, ages 10 and up, and a complexity of 1.66.

### 10. Santorini

**Santorini (Gordon Hamilton, credited as Gord!; Roxley edition 2016) is a game of moving and building on a 5×5 grid.** Each turn you move one of your two builders one space, then build a level next to it; a builder who climbs onto the third level wins, and a dome on top closes a space for good. The Roxley edition added a god powers variant that gives each player a unique way to break the rules. BoardGameGeek lists 2 to 4 players, ages 8 and up, about 20 minutes, with a complexity of 1.72; Wikipedia notes an all-white wooden edition published in 2004.
