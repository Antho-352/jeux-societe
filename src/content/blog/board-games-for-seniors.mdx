The best board games for seniors are the ones that are easy to see, easy to handle and quick to explain, and that leave room to talk. That rules out very few games for most older adults, but it helps to choose with the person in mind: their eyesight, their hands, their memory and who else will be at the table. Below are the criteria, then 12 games that meet them, from classics everyone knows to a few modern favorites. Several have free boards you can print large at home.

## What makes a game senior-friendly?

There is no "seniors' game": a retired chess player and a grandmother who has never played a board game need very different things. These five questions sort most games quickly.

- **Can everyone see it?** Large numbers, strong colors and a plain board beat small text and busy art. Cards with small print are the most common problem.
- **Can everyone handle it?** Thick tiles and big pieces are easier for stiff or shaky hands than small cards, tiny pegs or cubes. Tile racks and card holders help.
- **Can the rules be told in a minute?** Games people already know need no teaching at all. New games should fit in a few sentences.
- **Does it suit the group?** Some games are for two (checkers, backgammon, cribbage), others for a table of four to seven (Rummikub, Just One).
- **Is it social?** Short turns and a game that is still open at the end keep everyone involved; long silent thinking does not.

## Classic board games everyone knows

**A game the player already knows is the easiest game of all: no rules to learn, and often good memories attached.** These five are also the ones with free printables on our site.

**Checkers** is the safest first choice: two kinds of piece, one rule that matters (jumping is compulsory), and games of 10 to 30 minutes. Our [checkers rules](/rules/checkers/) include a printable board with pieces, and the one-page [printable checkerboard](/board-games/printable-checkerboard/) enlarges well (see the printing tips below). For players who want more, our guide on [how to win at checkers](/blog/how-to-win-at-checkers/) has diagrams to work through together.

<ItemCallout href="/board-games/printable-checkerboard/" note="A one-page 8×8 board. Print it at 129% on tabloid paper and use coins, bottle caps or large buttons as pieces." />

**Dominoes** suit almost everyone: you match the number of spots on one end of a tile to an open end on the table. A standard double-six set has 28 tiles, and the block and draw games on our [dominoes rules page](/rules/dominoes/) are for 2 to 4 players. Wooden or thick plastic tiles are easy to hold, and the pips can be counted rather than read.

**Backgammon** is a race around the board decided by two dice and a little judgment. Many older players learned it young; for others, the dice make it forgiving. Our [backgammon rules](/rules/backgammon/) cover setup and the doubling cube, and the [printable backgammon board](/board-games/printable-backgammon-board/) spreads over two Letter pages that tape together.

**Cribbage** is the card game for people who love counting fifteens and runs. The pegboard keeps the score, so nobody has to add up totals at the end. Our [cribbage rules](/rules/cribbage/) explain the deal, the crib and scoring, and come with a score sheet; the [printable cribbage board](/board-games/printable-cribbage-board/) replaces the wooden one.

**Chinese checkers** has the simplest moves of all (step or hop along the lines) and seats up to six, which makes it a good table game for a group. See our [Chinese checkers rules](/rules/chinese-checkers/).

## Word games

**Word games keep a table talking, and the best ones do not need fast reading.**

The **crossword word game** is a paper version of the tile-laying game made famous by Scrabble (designed by Alfred Mosher Butts, according to BoardGameGeek): a 15×15 board with bonus squares and 100 letter tiles. Our printable is free; print the board enlarged and cut the tiles from card stock, or use a set you already own.

<ItemCallout href="/board-games/printable-crossword-game-with-friends/" note="A free Scrabble-style board, 100 letter tiles and the rules on three Letter pages. Print the tiles on card stock." />

**Just One** (Ludovic Roudy and Bruno Sautter, 2018) won the Spiel des Jahres, the German game of the year award, in 2019. One player guesses a mystery word; everyone else writes a one-word clue, and identical clues cancel out. Everyone plays on the same team, 3 to 7 players, about 20 minutes, and it has the lowest weight in our list.

**Wits & Wagers** (Dominic Crapuchettes, 2005) asks questions with a number for an answer, such as a year or a distance. Everyone writes a guess, then players bet on whichever guess they think is closest without going over, so you can do well without knowing the answers. BoardGameGeek gives 3 to 7 players, and the game's description there says it accommodates up to 20 people in teams.

## Easy modern games

**Modern games bring something new to a regular game afternoon without long rulebooks.** These four have large components and short turns.

**Rummikub** (Ephraim Hertzano) is rummy with numbered tiles instead of cards: 104 tiles numbered 1 to 13 in four colors, plus two jokers, which you play in runs and groups. Tiles stand on racks, which is easier than holding a hand of cards. It won the Spiel des Jahres in 1980 and plays 2 to 4.

**Qwirkle** (Susan McKinley Ross, 2006) uses 108 wooden blocks in six colors and six shapes. Lay blocks in lines that share a color or a shape, and score for every block in the lines you touch. There is nothing to read, and it won the Spiel des Jahres in 2011. BoardGameGeek lists 2 to 4 players, ages 6 and up, about 45 minutes.

**Sequence** (Doug Reuter, 1982) combines cards with a board that shows every card twice. Play a card, put a chip on a matching space, and try to make a row of five. It seats 2 to 12; with more than three players, Wikipedia's summary of the rules splits everyone into two or three teams, so a stronger player can partner someone who is less sure.

## Games for people living with dementia

Games can still be part of life after a diagnosis of dementia. The NHS says that physical, mental, social and creative activities can help people live well with dementia and improve wellbeing, and that people can carry on with the activities they enjoy, though they may take longer than they used to. The Alzheimer's Association gives practical advice for carers that applies directly to games:

- **Stick with what the person has always enjoyed**, and adjust it to their abilities today.
- **Focus on enjoyment, not achievement.** Whether the score is right matters less than the time together.
- **Break the activity into simple steps**, one at a time, and help get it started.
- **Stop or change the activity** when attention wanes or frustration grows, and try again later.

In practice, that means familiar games with one simple action: matching dominoes by number, bingo with large cards, or a race game like [snakes and ladders](/rules/snakes-and-ladders/) where the dice do the deciding. Play in teams so nobody is on their own, skip the scoring if it causes stress, and keep games short. We do not make any claim that games slow memory loss; the point is a good afternoon together.

## Print boards large: tabloid and A3 tips

Our printable boards are made for US Letter (8.5 × 11 in) paper. **Enlarging them is the cheapest way to get bigger squares and bigger pieces.**

- **Letter to tabloid (11 × 17 in): print at 129%.** That is the largest scale that still fits the short side (11 ÷ 8.5 = 1.29). Many home printers cannot take tabloid paper, but print and copy shops can.
- **A4 to A3: print at 141%.** A3 is exactly twice the area of A4.
- **Or tile it**: print a one-page board in two halves at 200% and tape them together, the way our backgammon and chess boards already come.
- **Use card stock** so the board lies flat, and lay it on a tray or a non-slip mat.
- **Pick big, contrasting pieces**: coins, bottle caps, large buttons or checkers from an old set, dark on a light board.
- **Good light matters** as much as size: a lamp over the table, no glare on glossy paper.

Our [how to print](/how-to-print/) page covers paper, scaling and double-sided printing.
