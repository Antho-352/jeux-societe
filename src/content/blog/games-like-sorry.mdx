Sorry! is the game where you apologize and mean none of it: draw a card, land on a rival, send their pawn back to start. If your family loves that moment, you are in luck, because Sorry! has a whole family of relatives that do the same thing in a slightly different way. Below are nine of them, what each one changes, and which four you can print free tonight with nothing more than a die and a handful of buttons.

## Why are Sorry!, Trouble and Ludo the same game?

**They all descend from Pachisi, a race game from India.** BoardGameGeek describes Sorry! as a "Pachisi type game" and files it, with Trouble, Aggravation, Wahoo, Parcheesi and Mensch ärgere Dich nicht, in the same "Pachisi / Ludo" family. Wikipedia says Sorry! is based on Ludo. The rules are almost identical: four pawns leave a start area, race around a shared track and enter a private home lane; landing on a rival sends it back.

**Ludo is the English branch of Pachisi.** The Victoria and Albert Museum, which holds early boxed sets of both, says Pachisi has been played in India for many centuries and is considered its national game. It reached England at the end of the 19th century: the publisher John Jaques registered the name "Patchesi" in 1887, and the game became better known as Ludo.

Sorry! came about forty years later. William Henry Storey of Southend-on-Sea registered it as a trade mark in England in 1929, according to Wikipedia, and Parker Brothers took it up in the United States in 1934. Its big change was to replace the dice with a deck of cards.

## What makes Sorry! different from its cousins?

**The cards.** The modern deck has 45 cards: five 1s and four each of Sorry!, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11 and 12, with no 6 or 9 so nobody confuses them. You leave Start only with a 1, a 2 or a Sorry! card. A 4 moves you backwards, which is a shortcut: back up from just past Start and your pawn is a few squares from its Safety Zone. An 11 lets you swap places with a rival, and a Sorry! card takes a pawn from Start and bumps any rival pawn back.

The board adds slides: land on the start of one that is not your color and you glide to its end, sending every pawn on it back to Start. The last five squares before home are a Safety Zone where nobody can touch you. BoardGameGeek suggests a more strategic variant: deal five cards to each player and let them choose which to play.

## Which games like Ludo can you print free?

**Ludo and Pachisi are Sorry! without the box**, and both boards are on our site.

**Ludo** is the version most children learn first. Everyone shares one die; you need a six to bring a token out of your yard, then you race clockwise around 52 squares and up your colored home column. Land on a rival and it goes back to its yard. A six gives you another roll, and you need the exact number to reach the center. Our rules page also lists the house rules families add, such as blocks made of two tokens.

**Pachisi**, played in the United States under the brand name Parcheesi, is the richer version. You roll two dice and use them separately or together, a five brings a pawn out of the nest, and two pawns of one color form a blockade nobody can pass. Capturing a pawn earns a 20-space bonus, which gives older children real choices to make.

If you have a German friend, ask about **Mensch ärgere Dich nicht** ("Man, don't get angry"), Josef Friedrich Schmidt's 1907–08 version. BoardGameGeek reports about 70 million copies sold. It plays on a Ludo-style board with one die, but it has no safe squares anywhere, which makes it the meanest of the family.

## What are games like Trouble and Aggravation?

**These are the American marble branch of the family**, and the answer to "a game like Sorry! with marbles".

**Trouble** (1965) is the simplest. Its die sits in the Pop-O-Matic, a clear plastic bubble you press to roll, so the die never gets lost under the sofa. You need a six to move a peg from home to start, and a six also gives you another turn. BoardGameGeek credits Frank Kohner, Paul Kohner and Fred Kroll and rates it for 2 to 4 players aged 4 and up. In Britain the same game is sold as Frustration.

**Aggravation** (1962) is played with marbles on a board drilled with holes for up to six players. BoardGameGeek credits Harry W. First. Its twist is the shortcut: lucky rolls take a marble across the center of the board. You cannot pass your own marbles, and landing on a rival "aggravates" it back to base.

**Wahoo** is the folk game behind Aggravation. BoardGameGeek describes it as a popular American Southwest game of the early to mid-1900s, played by four to six in teams, with as many rule sets and board shapes as there are families. Agree on the rules before the first roll: BoardGameGeek notes that families play many variants, most of them with partners.

## Is there a game like Sorry! with playing cards?

**Yes: Pegs and Jokers.** It is the traditional game closest to Sorry!, because cards, not dice, move your pegs. According to BoardGameGeek, a five moves five spaces, a ten moves ten, an ace moves one and an eight moves backwards, just like Sorry!'s 4. A joker sends a rival peg home. You race five pegs, and it seats 2 to 8 players aged 7 and up.

**DOG** (Schmidt Spiele and others) takes the same idea further: players hold a hand of cards, play in partnerships and trade one card with their partner each round. Once one partner is home, their cards help the other. BoardGameGeek lists it for 2 to 6 players, ages 9 and up, about 45 minutes, which makes it the grown-up Sorry!.

## Which race games suit the youngest and the oldest?

**Sorry! is rated for ages 6 and up on BoardGameGeek.** These two cover the players on either side of that.

**Snakes and Ladders** is the race game for children who cannot yet choose between moves. There is one pawn each and no decision at all: roll, climb the ladders, slide down the snakes, be the first to 100. BoardGameGeek notes the game came to the United Kingdom from India and that Milton Bradley published it in the US as Chutes and Ladders in 1943. Our [snakes and ladders rules](/rules/snakes-and-ladders/) settle the exact-roll argument.

<ItemCallout href="/board-games/printable-snakes-and-ladders/" note="A colorful one-page board with squares 1 to 100. Add a die and a counter for each player." />

**Backgammon** is the race game adults return to. Two players move 15 checkers each in opposite directions with two dice. A single checker on a point is a "blot" that can be hit and sent back to the start, Sorry!-style, but two checkers make a safe point and a row of them makes a wall. The dice still matter, but the decisions matter more. Read the [backgammon rules](/rules/backgammon/) before your first game; for more two-player ideas, see our [board games for couples](/blog/board-games-for-couples/).
