Most people who search for games like Monopoly still like the idea of Monopoly: buy property, build, collect rent, get rich. What they don't like is the four-hour game that ends with two players sulking on the sofa. The ten games below keep the money and fix one problem each. Every player count, age and time comes from BoardGameGeek, and one of the ten is free to print tonight. A four-question quiz at the end picks one for your table.

## Why look for a Monopoly alternative?

**Most complaints about Monopoly come down to four things**, and all four are in the rules.

- **Length.** BoardGameGeek lists Monopoly at 60 to 180 minutes for 2 to 8 players. In the standard rules the game ends only when one player is left.
- **Player elimination.** A bankrupt player "must immediately retire from the game", says the Parker Brothers rulebook. The first one out can wait an hour or more.
- **Luck.** You roll two dice and move. Where you land decides most of what you can buy.
- **Too little real trading.** Deals between players are allowed, but a trade that completes a color group can decide the game, so players often refuse them.

Some of the length is homemade. The official rules say Free Parking pays nothing, and that a property the player declines goes to auction straight away. Paying tax money out on Free Parking pumps extra cash into the game, and skipping auctions leaves properties unsold: both house rules make it last longer.

<Callout type="tip" title="Or fix Monopoly itself">
The Parker Brothers rulebook includes a **short game of 60 to 90 minutes**: deal three free properties to each player, allow hotels after three houses, and stop at the first bankruptcy. The richest player wins. There is also a time-limit game: agree on an end time, and the richest player at that time wins.

## Which games like Monopoly are shorter?

**These four keep the money feeling and finish in half an hour or less.**

**For Sale** (Stefan Dorra, 1997) is a real estate game in two halves. First everyone bids coins for buildings. Then you sell those buildings for checks, with everyone playing a building at the same time. It plays 3 to 6 in about 30 minutes.

**Splendor** (Marc André, Space Cowboys, 2014) is a race to 15 prestige points. On your turn you take gem chips, buy a card with them, or reserve one. Each card you buy is a permanent discount on later cards, so your little business snowballs, which is exactly the Monopoly feeling without the dice. It plays 2 to 4 in about 30 minutes.

**Machi Koro** (Masao Suganuma, 2012) is the closest to Monopoly's own loop of dice, property and income. You roll one or two dice each turn, and every building whose number comes up pays its owner; some buildings even take coins from the player who rolled, which feels a lot like collecting rent. You spend the coins on more buildings and race to complete your landmarks. Nobody goes bankrupt, and it plays 2 to 4, ages 10 and up, in about 30 minutes.

### Games like Monopoly Deal

**Monopoly Deal** (Hasbro, 2008) is itself the best-known short Monopoly: a card game for 2 to 5 players aged 8 and up, about 15 minutes. You win by completing three property sets of different colors; action cards like Debt Collector and Deal Breaker let you steal them. If your family has worn it out, For Sale and Splendor are the natural next step: both are just as quick, with more decisions per turn and no stealing.

## Which games have no player elimination?

**In all eleven games on this list, everyone plays to the end.** These two go furthest in the other direction, because the scoring keeps losing players in the race.

**Acquire** (Sid Sackson, first published by 3M in 1963) is the closest thing to a grown-up Monopoly. You place tiles that found and grow hotel chains, and you buy shares in them. When two chains merge, the biggest shareholders of the swallowed chain collect bonuses, and everyone can sell or trade their shares. At the end the richest investor wins. It plays 2 to 6, ages 12 and up, in about 90 minutes.

**Power Grid** (Friedemann Friese, 2004) has you bid for power plants, buy coal, oil, garbage and uranium on a market, and connect cities to your network. There are no dice, and the turn order works against whoever is leading. It is the heaviest game here and the longest: 2 to 6 players, 12 and up, about two hours.

If you want an elimination-free game that is also short, Splendor (above) has no dice either.

## Which games have more trading and negotiation?

**If the best part of Monopoly is the haggling, these three put it at the center.**

**Catan** (Klaus Teuber, 1995) is the modern classic. Dice decide which tiles produce brick, wood, ore, wheat and sheep; you trade with the other players, and with the ports, to build roads, settlements and cities. The first to 10 points wins. It plays 3 to 4, ages 10 and up, in 60 to 120 minutes.

**Bohnanza** (Uwe Rosenberg, 1997) is a card game about bean farming where you may not reorder your hand. You must plant cards in the order you drew them, so you trade away what you cannot use. Every turn is a small market. BoardGameGeek lists it for 3 to 7 players, 45 minutes.

**Chinatown** (Karsten Hartwig, 1999) is the purest negotiation game here. Lots and business tiles are dealt at random, so the only way to complete a business is to trade. Every shop pays out each round, and complete businesses pay much better. It plays 3 to 5 in about an hour.

## Which free games like Monopoly can you print?

You do not have to buy a box. Two games in our catalogue keep the money and the building: one is given away in full, the other as a free demo.

**Unfair** is a theme-park building card game by Joel Finch. Over eight rounds you build attractions and upgrades, earn coins from your park, chase secret blueprints and play events at rival parks. Nobody is knocked out: after the last round, the most points wins. The publisher, 2am Gameworks, offers the complete base game free for personal use.

**Citadels** (Bruno Faidutti, 2000) is the shorter, lighter choice. ## Where did Monopoly come from? The Landlord's Game

**Monopoly began as a game designed to criticise monopolies.** Its inventor was Lizzie Magie, a follower of the economist Henry George, who argued for a single tax on land values. According to the British Library's curator of historical patents, Magie created The Landlord's Game by the end of 1903, and US patent 748,626 was granted on January 5, 1904.

The patent board already looks familiar: a continuous path around the edge, wages for passing the start, four railroads, a "go to jail" corner and a public park where Free Parking would later be. Magie later gave the game two sets of rules. Under the monopolist rules you tried to drive the others out of business. Under the anti-monopoly rules, later called **Prosperity**, everyone gained when wealth was created.

The game spread mostly as homemade copies, among college students and then among Quakers, who added fixed prices and Atlantic City street names. Charles Darrow learned it at a friend's house in 1932, sold his version as his own invention, and Parker Brothers bought it in 1935. That year Parker Brothers also bought Magie's 1924 patent for $500, with no royalties. Darrow's story lasted for decades: the 1990s Parker Brothers rulebook still opens with it.

<Callout type="note" title="Who is credited today?">
BoardGameGeek now lists both Charles Darrow and Elizabeth J. Magie (Phillips) as designers of Monopoly, and lists The Landlord's Game as its own entry, for 2 to 8 players.
</Callout>
