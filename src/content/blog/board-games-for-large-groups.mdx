Most board games stop at four or five players, and a lot of boxes that say "up to 8" are not much fun at 8. This list keeps only games that BoardGameGeek players still recommend at large counts, sorted by group size: 8 to 10, 11 to 12, and 13 to 30. Each pick has its player range, time and weight, and five of them have free files you can print as many times as you need. A quiz at the end matches a game to your group.

## What makes a board game work for a large group?

**A good big-group game keeps everyone busy at the same time.** In a turn-by-turn game, a ten-player table means waiting through nine turns for each one of yours. The games below avoid that in one of three ways:

- **Teams.** Codenames, Wavelength and Captain Sonar split the group in two, so a 10-player game is really a two-sided game where everyone talks.
- **Hidden roles.** In Secret Hitler, Werewolf or Two Rooms and a Boom, the game is the conversation: who is lying? Nobody waits, because everyone is watching everyone.
- **Simultaneous play.** In Telestrations, every player draws or guesses at once, and the round lasts as long for 12 players as for 4.

**Watch out for player elimination.** In classic Werewolf, a player knocked out early can sit out most of an hour. One Night Ultimate Werewolf and Blood on the Clocktower fix this: the first has a single vote and no elimination, and in the second dead players stay in the game as ghosts.

## Which board games work for 8 to 10 players?

**These seven are at their best around 8, and four of them go to 10.**

**Codenames** (Vlaada Chvátil, Czech Games Edition, 2015) splits the room into two teams around 25 word cards. Each team's spymaster gives one word and a number, such as "ocean, 3", and teammates try to touch their own agents without hitting the assassin. BoardGameGeek voters rate it best at 6 and 8 and still recommend it above 8, because extra players simply join a team. If your group already loves it, our list of [games like Codenames](/blog/games-like-codenames/) has twelve more clue games.

**Telestrations** (The Op Games, 2009) is the telephone game on paper. Everyone draws a secret word in their booklet and passes it; the next player guesses the drawing, the next draws that guess, and so on. The reveal of how "this" became "that" is the fun. The box seats 4 to 8 and voters rate it best at 8. The **12 Player Party Pack** takes it to 12.

**Captain Sonar** (Roberto Fraga and Yohan Lemonnier, Matagot, 2016) is the one to pick for a competitive 8. Two teams of up to four each run a submarine: the captain steers and announces moves, the radio operator tracks the enemy sub from the other captain's orders, and the crew prepare torpedoes, mines and other systems. In real-time mode, both teams play at once. It is the heaviest game in this section (2.21), for ages 14 and up, and best with exactly 8.

**Dixit** (Jean-Louis Roubira, Libellud, 2008) is the gentle choice. The storyteller describes one of their picture cards, everyone else slips in a card that fits, and then all vote. The box says 3 to 8, but voters rate it best at 5 or 6, so treat 8 as its upper limit.

<ItemCallout href="/party-games/dixit-print-and-play/" note="Asmodee's free Dixit file: rules and illustrated cards to print in color. The demo may hold fewer cards than the boxed game." />

For 8 to 10 players who like to lie, there are three hidden-role classics.

- **Secret Hitler** (Mike Boxleiter, Tommy Maranges and Max Temkin, 2016): liberals and hidden fascists elect a president and chancellor, who pass laws from a random deck. Voters rate it best at 8 and 10. It is free to print under a Creative Commons licence.
- **The Resistance: Avalon** (Don Eskridge, Indie Boards & Cards, 2012): Arthur's knights vote on quest teams while Mordred's minions sabotage them, and only Merlin knows who is evil. Best at 7 or 8.
- **One Night Ultimate Werewolf** (Ted Alspach and Akihisa Okui, Bézier Games, 2014): one night, one morning, one vote, about 10 minutes. No moderator and no elimination, which makes it the easiest werewolf game to start a party with.

<ItemCallout href="/party-games/secret-hitler/" note="The official print-and-play set, 13 pages of boards, policy tiles and cards, plus the 7-page rules. 5 to 10 players, ages 13 and up." />

## Which games work for 11 or 12 players?

**At 11 or 12, choose a game with two teams or one shared puzzle.**

**Wavelength** (Alex Hague, Justin Vickers and Wolfgang Warsch, 2019) has two teams guess where a hidden target sits on a dial between two opposites, such as hot and cold. The team's psychic gives one clue ("salad" for slightly cold), and the team argues about where to turn the dial. It plays 2 to 12, and voters recommend every count from 4 to 12.

**Concept** (Alain Rivollet and Gaëtan Beaujannot, Repos Production, 2013) is a guessing game without words. Two players place markers on a board of icons (liquid, white, food) until someone shouts "milk". Everyone else guesses at once, so 12 players are no slower than 6.

<ItemCallout href="/party-games/concept-print-and-play-demo/" note="Asmodee's free Concept demo: the icon board, word cards and rules. 4 to 12 players from age 10." />

**Deception: Murder in Hong Kong** (Tobey Ho, Jolly Thinkers, 2014) is a murder mystery in 20 minutes for 4 to 12. One player is the Forensic Scientist, who knows the answer but may only point to clue tiles; one of the investigators is secretly the murderer, and an accomplice may help them. Voters recommend it from 5 to 12, for ages 14 and up.

**Meow!** (Peter C. Hayward, Jellybean Games, 2019) is the silliest game here: a card game "for 3-12 cats", says its publisher. Every card is a secret rule, and you win by playing a turn without breaking any rule in other players' hands. Expect hissing and pretend paw-licking. Jellybean Games allows the files to be shared, so we host the rulebook.

<ItemCallout href="/card-games/meow/" note="Two printable decks, Kitten Cabal for families and The Cult of Cat, plus the rules. 3 to 12 players from age 6, 5 to 10 minutes." />

**Above 12, almost every game that works is a hidden-role game or a team game played across a room.**

**Two Rooms and a Boom** (Alan Gerding and Sean McCoy, Tuesday Knight Games, 2013) needs two rooms and plays 6 to 30. The Blue Team protects the President, the Red Team wants its Bomber in the President's room when time runs out. Each round, every room picks a leader who sends hostages to the other room, and rounds get shorter. BoardGameGeek voters recommend it from 8 up and rate it best between 14 and 22. The publisher's Print-n-Play is free.

<ItemCallout href="/party-games/two-rooms-and-a-boom/" note="Link to Tuesday Knight Games' free Print-n-Play. 6 to 30 players from age 8, rounds of a few minutes." />

**Werewolf** (Dimitry Davidoff, 1986), also called Mafia, is public domain. A moderator runs the nights, when the werewolves secretly pick a victim and the seer checks one player, and the days, when the village votes someone out. BoardGameGeek lists 8 to 24 players and voters rate it best at 13. You can deal the roles from an ordinary deck: decide that two aces are werewolves and one king is the seer, for example. A [printable playing card deck](/card-games/printable-playing-card-deck/) does the job.

**Blood on the Clocktower** (Steven Medway, The Pandemonium Institute, 2022) is Werewolf rebuilt for long evenings. A Storyteller runs the game for 5 to 20 players, every player has a different character with an ability, and dead players keep playing as ghosts. It is by far the heaviest pick (3.02), and voters rate it best at 9 to 12.

**Monikers** (Alex Hague and Justin Vickers, 2015) is a team game for 4 to 16 based on Celebrities, a public-domain parlor game. The same deck of names is used three times: first you may say anything except the name, then only one word, then only gestures. BoardGameGeek lists it for ages 17 and up, and voters recommend even counts above 12, so teams stay level. You can play Celebrities with slips of paper in a bowl and the timer below.

## Which free games can a big group play tonight?

**Free files matter more for a big group**: printing is cheap, and you can print a second copy for a second table. Besides the five games with free files above, there is [Printable Quick on the Draw](/party-games/printable-quick-on-the-draw/), Pictionary-style team scorecards for 4 to 10 players, and [Hidden Panda](/party-games/hidden-panda/), a 20-minute social deduction game for 5 to 8. More are on our [free party games](/party-games/) page.

Three classics work with a crowd and need nothing to print:

- **[Spoons](/rules/spoons/)**: everyone passes cards at once to collect four of a kind, then grabs one of the spoons, always one fewer than players. One deck seats 13; shuffle in a second for more.
- **[Bunco](/rules/bunco/)**: the club-night dice game, made for 12 players at three tables of four, with partners changing every round.
- **[Left Right Center](/rules/left-right-center/)**: roll three dice and pass chips left, right or to the center. No decisions, so the youngest player competes on equal terms; our rules page gives 3 to 12 players.

## How do you run a game night for 15 or more people?

**Split the group before the games come out.** Two tables of seven or eight usually beat one table of fifteen, unless the game is built for the crowd.

1. **Start with a team game everyone can join**, like Wavelength or Codenames: late arrivals simply join a team.
2. **Put the long game at its own table.** Blood on the Clocktower or Captain Sonar needs players who will stay for the whole game.
3. **Name a host for hidden-role games.** Werewolf and Blood on the Clocktower need a moderator or Storyteller who knows the rules well.
4. **End with something loud and short**: a round of Spoons, Meow! or One Night Ultimate Werewolf takes 10 minutes.
