There are dozens of types of solitaire, and most need nothing more than one ordinary deck of cards. Klondike, the game Windows made famous, is only the best known. The twelve below are classics you can deal on a kitchen table, from FreeCell, where almost every deal can be won, to Clock, which plays itself and comes out once in thirteen games. For each one you get the layout, the goal, and how often it can be won, from published studies and our own simulations. Spider, which needs two decks, and free print-and-play solo games come after.

## What counts as solitaire (patience)?

**Solitaire is any card game for one player; in Britain it is called patience.** In most of them you start from a layout (the tableau), move cards according to building rules, and try to sort the deck onto four foundation piles, one per suit, from ace to king. Others ask you to remove cards in pairs, or to squeeze the deck into one pile. Researchers Charlie Blake and Ian Gent note that patience games have been popular for more than 200 years, and that names are confused: in their words, Klondike is often just called "Patience" or "Solitaire", and is sometimes even called Canfield, which is a different game.

A few terms used below:

- **Tableau**: the cards laid out on the table, usually in columns.
- **Foundations**: the piles you build up by suit, ace to king, to win.
- **Stock and waste**: the undealt cards, and the pile where you turn them.
- **Building down in alternating colors**: a red 9 on a black 10, as in Klondike.

## Classic one-deck games, from Klondike to Aces Up

### 1. Klondike

**The game most people mean by "solitaire".** Seven columns of 1 to 7 cards, only the top card of each face up; 24 cards in the stock. Build down in alternating colors in the tableau, and up by suit on the foundations. You turn cards from the stock one or three at a time.

Klondike is a game of skill and luck. With every card known, Blake and Gent found 82% of draw-three deals and 90% of draw-one deals winnable. Real players see only the face-up cards: Wikipedia cites an experiment in which a skilled player won 189 of 442 games (43%). Our [Klondike rules page](/rules/klondike-solitaire/) covers the deal, draw one or three, and tips.

<ItemCallout href="/rules/klondike-solitaire/" note="How to deal the seven piles, draw one or draw three, the foundations, scoring and tips for first games." />

### 2. FreeCell

**Every card is face up from the start, which makes FreeCell a puzzle rather than a gamble.** Deal all 52 cards into eight columns (four of seven cards, four of six). Four free cells each hold one card. Build down in alternating colors, move one card at a time, and build the foundations up by suit.

Almost every deal can be won. According to Michael Keller's FreeCell FAQ, all of the 32,000 deals in the original Windows version except number 11982 have been solved, and Blake and Gent put the winnable share at 99.999%. FreeCell goes back to Paul Alfille, who wrote the first computer version in 1978 on the PLATO educational system, according to the FAQ and Wikipedia.

### 3. Spiderette (one-deck Spider)

**Spider's rules on a Klondike layout.** Wikipedia describes Spiderette as Spider with one pack, dealt in seven columns of 1 to 7 cards. As in Spider, you may build down on any suit, but only runs in one suit move as a unit, and a complete run from king to ace in one suit leaves the table. When you are stuck, deal one card from the stock onto each column. With every card known, Blake and Gent found 99.6% of deals winnable; with face-down cards, you will win less often.

### 4. Pyramid

**Pair cards that add up to 13.** Deal 28 cards in a pyramid of seven rows, each row overlapping the one above. Jacks count 11, queens 12, kings 13, so kings go alone. Only uncovered cards may be paired, with each other or with the top card of the stock or waste. You win by clearing the pyramid and the stock. Wikipedia puts the odds at about 1 in 50 under the strictest rules; the relaxed version only asks you to clear the pyramid.

### 5. Golf

**A fast game where you build up or down by one, regardless of suit.** Deal seven columns of five cards, all face up, and one card to start the foundation pile. Move the top card of any column onto the foundation if it is one higher or one lower; nothing goes on a king, and in strict Golf there is no wrapping from king to ace. When stuck, turn a card from the stock. Wikipedia describes a score of one point per card left over nine deals, like holes of golf. With every card known (including the stock), only 45% of deals can be cleared, so a low score is a good result.

### 6. Clock

**No decisions: the cards decide.** Deal twelve piles of four in a circle, like the hours on a clock, and four in the middle. Turn the top card of the middle pile and slide it under the pile of its hour (ace = 1 o'clock, jack = 11, queen = 12), then turn the top card of that pile, and so on. Kings go to the middle. You lose if the fourth king appears while cards are still face down.

Wikipedia and Blake and Gent both give the chance of winning as exactly 1 in 13, a result proved by Jenkyns and Muller in 1981. **We dealt 100,000 shuffled decks with a short program: 7,639 came out, or 7.6%**, against 7.7% for 1 in 13. Clock is the one solitaire you can play while talking, which makes it good for young children.

### 7. Accordion

**Squeeze the whole deck into one pile.** Lay the cards in one row, left to right. A pile may go onto its neighbor on the left, or onto the third pile to its left, when their top cards match in suit or rank; the whole pile moves with its top card. Wikipedia cites Bicycle and Hoyle for odds of about 1 in 100 when cards are dealt one at a time, but Blake and Gent found almost every deal winnable (99.9995%) when you can see all 52 cards before you start. It needs a long table, or a long floor.

### 8. Aces Up

**Discard everything but the aces.** Deal four cards in a row. Discard any card that is lower than another visible card of the same suit (aces are high), move any top card into an empty pile, and deal four more cards across when stuck. You win with only the four aces left. Wikipedia gives about 1 in 35 with normal play. **Our own simulation of 100,000 deals, using a simple rule for filling spaces, won only 1.5% (1 in 68)**: in Aces Up, the choice of which card fills a space is the whole skill. It needs only four piles of space.

## Which solitaires can be won most often?

**In open games, where every card is face up from the start, nothing is hidden, so every winnable deal is within reach of careful play**: FreeCell (99.999%) and Beleaguered Castle (68%) are the two in this list. Games with hidden cards mix luck with skill, and the gap between "winnable" and "won" is wide. Klondike is the clearest case: 82% of draw-three deals can be won by someone who knows every card, but a skilled human won 43% in the experiment Wikipedia cites.

The rules matter as much as the layout. Turning one card at a time instead of three raises Klondike's winnable share from 82% to 90%, according to Blake and Gent. Accordion and Aces Up, above, show the gap between "could be won" and "is won" even more clearly.

## Harder one-deck solitaires: Canfield, Yukon, Scorpion, Beleaguered Castle

### 9. Canfield (Demon)

**The classic "almost" game.** Deal 13 cards face down as a reserve and turn the top one up; the next card sets the starting rank for all four foundations; four cards start the tableau. Build down in alternating colors, turning the corner from ace to king; turn the stock three at a time. Wikipedia describes it as a game with a very low probability of winning, first recorded in England in 1891 as Demon Patience, and later linked to the gambler Richard A. Canfield. Even with every card known, Blake and Gent found only 71% of deals winnable.

### 10. Yukon

**Klondike without a stock.** All 52 cards are dealt into seven columns of 1, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11 cards, the top five of each column face up (the first column is a single face-up card). You may move any face-up card along with everything on top of it, as long as the card you move goes onto the next higher card of the other color.

### 11. Scorpion

**Yukon-style moves, built by suit.** Deal seven columns of seven: the first four have three cards face down under four face up, the other three are all face up, and three cards are kept aside to deal later. Any face-up card can move with the cards above it onto the next higher card of the same suit. Build four complete suits from king down to ace in the tableau to win.

### 12. Beleaguered Castle

**FreeCell without cells.** Take out the four aces as foundations, then deal the rest in eight rows of six, four on each side of the aces. Move the top card of a row to a foundation or onto a card one higher in any row, regardless of suit; any card may fill an empty row. Every card is visible, and Blake and Gent found 68% of deals winnable. Wikipedia notes that it was first recorded in 1883.

## Two-deck and harder solitaires: Spider

**Spider is the famous two-deck solitaire.** Bicycle's rules deal ten columns from two shuffled decks, the first cards face down and the top cards face up, with the rest kept as a stock. Build down regardless of suit; a run of the same suit moves as one unit; a complete suit from king to ace leaves the table. When stuck, deal one card onto each column, but only once no column is empty. You win when all eight suits are cleared.

Wikipedia cites Hoyle's for winning about 1 game in 3 with good play. Easier versions use one or two suits only; Spiderette, above, is the one-deck version.

## Modern solo card games you can print free

If you have played every patience in the book, solo games designed in the last few years bring new goals: a lock to pick, a poem to write, a dungeon to survive. Several are free to print, and two of them use an ordinary deck of cards.

**Picklock!** (Steven Tondeur) needs only a standard deck. You probe the four pins of a lock to find the order they bind in, then lift each one to its exact height before time runs out, while keeping the noise down. It placed 13th overall in the BoardGameGeek 2022 Solitaire Print and Play Contest and plays in about 10 minutes.

<ItemCallout href="/card-games/picklock/" note="A ten-minute solo lock-picking game for a standard 52-card deck. Free rules to print." />

**Ripples and Branches** (Matt Hanna) turns a deck with jokers into a poet's walk between village, field, forest and stream. You collect inspiration and deliver a haiku of 5, 7 and 5 syllables before the deck runs out. It won Most Original Theme in the same 2022 contest.

<ItemCallout href="/card-games/ripples-and-branches-a-solo-game-of-haiku/" note="A solo game for a standard deck with jokers and one token. About 20 minutes." />

**Orchard** (Mark Tuck) uses just nine printed cards and some dice: overlap fruit trees on matching fruit to score. It won the 2018 9-Card Nanogame Print and Play Design Contest and plays in under ten minutes.

<ItemCallout href="/card-games/orchard-9-card-solitaire/" note="Nine cards, a few dice, ten minutes: a free solo card-laying game." />

For longer sessions, **Mini Rogue** is a nine-card dungeon dive with dice, and **Bismarck's Dance** a 30 to 45 minute historical card game about German diplomacy from 1870 to 1890.
