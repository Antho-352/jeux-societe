UNO is easy to love and easy to wear out: after the hundredth Draw Two, most families want something that feels familiar but plays differently. The twelve games below start with UNO's own ancestors, which need nothing but a normal deck, then move to its Mattel cousins, to faster party card games, and to three free card games you can print tonight. For each one you get the deck you need, the player count and what changes compared with UNO. A short quiz at the end picks one for you.

## Why is UNO really Crazy Eights?

**UNO is a branded version of a much older family of card games.** BoardGameGeek's entry for UNO says it plainly: "UNO is a commercial version of Crazy Eights, a public domain card game played with a standard deck of playing cards." Wikipedia places it in the same family, the shedding games (the first player to get rid of all their cards wins).

The brand itself began in 1971, when Merle Robbins designed the game in Reading, Ohio. According to Wikipedia, his family mortgaged their home to raise $8,000 for 5,000 copies, which he first sold from his barbershop. He later sold the rights for $50,000 plus 10 cents a game to a group led by Robert Tezak, who founded International Games to market it. International Games became part of Mattel in 1992.

**Put the two games side by side and the family likeness is obvious.** In Crazy Eights you match the top card by suit or rank, and eights are wild: you name the next suit. The most common house rules for Crazy Eights, listed by Wikipedia and on our [Crazy Eights rules page](/rules/crazy-eights/), are queens skip, aces reverse and twos make the next player draw two. That is UNO's Skip, Reverse and Draw Two, on an ordinary deck.

## Which games like UNO can you play with a normal deck?

These three are UNO's closest relatives, and they cost nothing if you already own a pack of cards. No pack? Print our [free playing card deck](/card-games/printable-playing-card-deck/) on card stock.

**Crazy Eights** is the direct ancestor. Deal five cards each (seven with two players), turn one face up, and match it by suit or rank. An eight can go on anything and lets you name a new suit. BoardGameGeek rates it for ages 4 and up, younger than UNO, because there are no action cards to remember unless you add them.

<ItemCallout href="/rules/crazy-eights/" note="The full rules, scoring, and the variants that turn it into UNO: queens skip, aces reverse, twos draw two." />

**Mau-Mau** is the German branch of the family. Wikipedia says it appeared in Germany shortly after the Second World War, with its rules first published in 1958. It uses a 32-card pack (a standard deck with the 2s to 6s removed): a seven makes the next player draw two, an eight skips them, and a jack is wild. When you play your last card you must say "Mau", or "Mau-Mau" if it is a jack.

**Switch** is the British and Irish version, also called Black Jack or, in New Zealand, Last Card. Aces change the suit, a two makes the next player pick up two unless they play a two of their own (then the next player picks up four), and a black jack means picking up seven unless you answer with a red jack. Forget to say "last card" and you draw two.

**Mao** takes the same matching game and hides the rules. On BoardGameGeek's description, new players are told only that the aim is to get rid of their cards; every time you break a rule you take a penalty card, and in some variants the winner adds a new secret rule for the next round. It suits teenagers and adults with patience for chaos.

## Are Phase 10 and Skip-Bo like UNO?

**They share UNO's look and its shelf, not its rules.** Both use numbered, coloured cards with Skip or wild cards, and BoardGameGeek lists Mattel among the publishers of each.

**Phase 10** is a rummy game. Ken Johnson's 1982 game deals ten cards each, and you draw and discard while trying to complete a combination, the "phase": two sets of three, then a set and a run, and so on up to Phase 10. Only one phase per hand counts, so a player who misses repeats it. The deck has 108 cards, including four Skips and eight Wilds. BoardGameGeek lists 45 minutes, but reaching Phase 10 takes at least ten hands; the official sheet includes shorter variations.

<ItemCallout href="/rules/phase-10/" note="All ten phases, how to lay them down and hit, scoring, and the shorter official variations." />

**Skip-Bo** is a race to empty your own stock pile. BoardGameGeek credits Hazel "Skip" Bowman and dates it to 1967, and notes it is based on the traditional game Spite and Malice. Players build shared piles from 1 to 12, and the Skip-Bo cards are wild. It feels like UNO because you want to get rid of cards, but the real skill is in organising your four discard piles.

## Which card games are faster and meaner than UNO?

If the best part of UNO is hitting the next player with a Draw Four, these three keep the fun of an attack and add something new.

**Sleeping Queens** (Miranda Evarts, Gamewright, 2005) is the gentle one, for two to five players aged 8 and up. Twelve queens lie face down; you play a king to wake one, a knight to steal someone else's, or a sleeping potion to put one back to sleep. Dragons and wands block those attacks, which gives children their first taste of defending. Like UNO, you can also discard number cards to draw new ones.

**Exploding Kittens** (Matthew Inman, Elan Lee and Shane Small, 2015) turns the draw pile into a threat. BoardGameGeek calls it "a kitty-powered version of Russian Roulette": players draw until someone pulls an Exploding Kitten and loses. Your cards let you peek at the deck, shuffle it or force the next player to draw more. Two to five players, 15 minutes, ages 7 and up.

**Taco Cat Goat Cheese Pizza** (Dave Campbell, 2018) keeps UNO's goal, emptying your hand first, but replaces matching with speed. Each player lays a card while saying the next word of "taco, cat, goat, cheese, pizza"; when the card matches the word, everyone slaps the pile and the last hand down picks it up. Gorilla, narwhal and groundhog cards add gestures before the slap. It seats two to eight.

**Spot It!** drops turns altogether. Its 55 cards each carry eight symbols, and any two cards share exactly one. The first player to spot the match gains or gets rid of a card, depending on the mini-game. It plays two to eight from age 6, and no reading is needed.

<ItemCallout href="/card-games/spot-it-dobble-print-and-play-demo/" note="Asmodee's free Spot It! demo kit: a small deck to print before you buy the tin." />

For a slap game with a normal deck, [Slapjack](/rules/slapjack/) is the traditional version.

## Which free card games like UNO can you print?

**Three complete games from Jellybean Games play in five to ten minutes**, use one shared rule, and suit children from age 6: play a card for its number, as treasure, or for its action, and be the first to 21 points. They feel like UNO because every card is either a number or an attack.

**Scuttle!** (Peter C. Hayward, 2016) is the pirate one. On your turn you draw a card or play one: as treasure, or for its effect, like stealing a chest or wiping the board. Four players can play in two teams, and there is a solo mode against a "Robot Pirate". The publisher allows rehosting, so the cards and rulebook are on our page.

<ItemCallout href="/card-games/scuttle/" note="6 Letter card sheets and a 10-page rulebook in colour, for 1 to 5 players. Print on card stock and cut." />

**Ninjitsu!** (2018) is its sequel and adds bluffing: any card can be played face down as a secret. It still counts towards your 21, but opponents can spend a turn stealing it, and some secrets are booby-trapped.

**[Brains!](/card-games/brains/)** (2019) completes the set with zombies and a personal graveyard, and all three decks can be shuffled together.
