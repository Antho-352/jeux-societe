## What you need

A morris board: three squares, one inside the other, joined by lines through the middle of each side. The 24 places where lines meet or turn are the **points**. Each player has nine pieces, called **men**, in their own colour. Our printable board includes 18 men to cut out; coins of two kinds work just as well.

The game dates back at least to the Roman Empire and has many names: mill, merels, morris. In German-speaking countries it is still a household classic called *Mühle*.

## Setup

The board starts empty. Each player takes their nine men in hand. Decide who plays first, usually by lot, and alternate the first player between games.

## How to play

The game has three phases.

### Phase 1: placing

Players take turns placing one man on any empty point. If you place a man so that three of your men stand in a straight line along one of the drawn lines, you have formed a **mill**. Forming a mill lets you immediately remove one of your opponent's men from the board. You may not take a man that is part of an opponent's mill unless every one of their men is in a mill.

Removed men are out of the game. The placing phase ends when both players have put all nine men down (or as many as survive).

### Phase 2: moving

Players now take turns sliding one of their men along a line to an **adjacent** empty point. Men cannot jump. Every time a move forms a new mill, you remove one enemy man, with the same restriction as before.

You may "open" a mill by moving a man out of it and close it again on a later turn by moving it back. Each time it closes, it counts as a new mill.

### Phase 3: flying

When a player is down to **three** men, those men may "fly": on each move, one man can go to any empty point on the board, not only an adjacent one. Some older rule books treat flying as optional; most modern players use it.

## Scoring and winning

You win when your opponent:

- has only **two** men left, so they can never form a mill again; or
- cannot make a legal move on their turn because all their men are blocked.

If neither side can make progress, the players may agree a draw. With perfect play, computers have shown the game itself is a draw.

## Common variants

- **No flying**: the third phase is skipped and three men move like any others.
- **Three men's morris**: three men each on a 3×3 grid; place them as in tic-tac-toe, then slide them until someone makes a line of three.
- **Six men's morris**: six men each, played without the outer square. No flying. Popular in medieval Europe.
- **Twelve men's morris**: four diagonal lines are added and each player has twelve men, so the board can fill up during placing, which is a draw. In South Africa it is played as the sport *morabaraba*.
- **Lasker morris**: devised by chess champion Emanuel Lasker. Ten men each, and from the start you may either place a new man or move one already on the board.

## Tips for first games

- During placing, spread your men to versatile points such as the middle of each side, rather than rushing to make a mill.
- A man on a crossing point (where four lines meet) has more moves later than one in a corner.
- Aim for a "running mill": two mills that share a gap, so that one move opens one mill and closes the other, removing a man every turn.
- Block your opponent's two-in-a-row before it becomes a mill, especially in the moving phase.
- Count the men. Once you reach three, flying makes you dangerous again, so do not give up.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I remove a man that is in a mill?** Only if every one of your opponent's men is in a mill.

**Does forming two mills at once remove two men?** Rule books differ on this, so agree before you start.

**Can I use the same mill twice?** Yes. Open it on one turn and close it on a later turn to score again.

**What happens if I cannot move?** You lose, unless you are allowed to fly.

**Do diagonal lines count?** Not on the standard board, which has none. Twelve men's morris adds diagonals.
