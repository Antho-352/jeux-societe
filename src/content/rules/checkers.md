## What you need

An 8×8 board with alternating light and dark squares, the same board as chess, and 24 flat round pieces: 12 of one colour and 12 of another. Tournament sets are red and white; black and red is common in the United States. Our printable has the board and 24 pieces to cut out.

## Setup

Turn the board so that each player has a dark square in the near left corner. Only the 32 dark squares are used. Each player sets 12 pieces (called **men**) on the dark squares of the three rows nearest them, leaving the two middle rows empty. The row nearest each player is their **king row**.

The player with the darker pieces moves first. In the official rules the first game is drawn by lot and colours alternate in later games.

## How to play

### Ordinary moves

On your turn you move one piece one square diagonally forward onto an empty dark square. Men never move backwards until they become kings.

### Jumps (captures)

If a piece of yours stands diagonally next to an enemy piece and the square directly beyond it is empty, you capture by jumping over that piece and removing it. If, after landing, the same piece can jump again, you must continue in the same turn; the jumps may change direction. All captured pieces come off the board at the end of the sequence.

Two rules trip up beginners:

- **Jumping is compulsory.** If you can capture, you must. If several captures are available you may choose any of them, not necessarily the one that takes the most pieces, but once you start a multiple jump you must finish it.
- A piece may not be jumped twice in the same sequence.

### Kings

A man that reaches the far king row is crowned by placing a second piece on top of it (or flipping it, if your set has crowns on the back). Reaching the king row ends the move, even if another jump looks possible; the new king only moves on your next turn. Kings move and capture one square diagonally in any direction, forwards or backwards. Any piece may capture a king.

## Scoring and winning

You win when your opponent cannot move on their turn, either because all their pieces are captured or because the ones that remain are blocked. The game is a draw if neither side can force a win, usually agreed when both players have kings and nothing is happening; in tournament play a referee can declare a draw after 40 moves without progress.

## Common variants

- **Three-move restriction**: in championship play the first three moves are drawn at random from a list of openings to reduce draws. Go-as-you-please (free openings) is what everyone plays at home.
- **Huffing**: an old rule, no longer in official play, where a player who misses a capture loses the piece that should have jumped instead of being made to take it.
- **Jumping in the same turn after crowning** is a common house rule; official rules say the move ends on the king row.
- **International draughts** is a different game on a 10×10 board with 20 pieces each. Men may capture backwards, kings "fly" any distance along a diagonal, and you must take the sequence that captures the most pieces. It is the game of the World Draughts Federation (FMJD) and the standard in France, the Netherlands and Russia.

## Tips for first games

- Keep your back row filled as long as you can; it stops the opponent from crowning.
- Because captures are compulsory, you can offer one piece to drag an enemy man out of position and jump two in return.
- Move in groups. A lone piece in the centre is easy to trap.
- A king is worth about two men; getting the first king usually decides a game between beginners.
- Count the pieces before trading when ahead: even exchanges favour the side with more material.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to jump if it loses me a piece?** Yes. Captures are compulsory even when the reply costs you more.

**Can a man jump backwards?** Not in English draughts. Only kings move and capture backwards. (In international draughts men do capture backwards.)

**Can I keep jumping after I reach the king row?** No. Crowning ends your turn; the king starts moving on your next move.

**What is the difference between checkers and draughts?** None in the rules: "checkers" is the American name and "draughts" the British one for the same 8×8 game.

**Who moves first?** The player with the darker pieces (red in tournament sets, black in most US sets).
